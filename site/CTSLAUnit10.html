Orientation: A Professor and Twenty-Seven Years

Orientación: Un profesor y veintisiete años

In 1863, the year Lincoln signed the Emancipation Proclamation and Lee invaded Pennsylvania, a forty-one-year-old Methodist professor of Hebrew and Greek at Drew Theological Seminary in New Jersey began a project so vast that his colleagues thought he had lost his senses. His name was James Strong. His project was to take every single word of the English King James Bible — every the, every and, every God, every love, every righteousness, every kyrios and every dikaiosynê — and trace it back to the exact Hebrew or Greek word behind it, then assign each Hebrew word and each Greek word a unique number that any English-only reader could use. He wanted to put the original languages in the hand of the plowboy.

It took him twenty-seven years. He worked nights and weekends while teaching full-time at Drew. He recruited over a hundred volunteer assistants. He cross-checked every entry against multiple ancient manuscripts. When Strong's Exhaustive Concordance of the Bible finally went to press in 1890, it was the size of a city telephone directory. It listed every English word in the KJV in alphabetical order, every verse where that word appeared, and beside each verse the unique number that pointed to the Hebrew or Greek word underneath. The Hebrew side ran from H1 (av, "father") to H8674. The Greek side ran from G1 (Alpha) to G5624. In the back of the book were two dictionaries — a Hebrew lexicon and a Greek lexicon — arranged numerically. Any English-speaking believer could now look up a verse, see the Strong's number beside the word, flip to the back, and read what the Hebrew or Greek actually meant. The seminary had been moved into the hand of the layman.

For 1,800 years the Hebrew and Greek originals had been locked behind seminary walls. In 1890 a Methodist professor handed the key to anyone who would pick it up.

The Wycliffe-Tyndale tradition

Strong did not invent democratizing Scripture. He stood on a long line. John Wycliffe in the 1380s translated the whole Bible into Middle English so that every English peasant could read it. William Tyndale in the 1520s did the same for early modern English — and was strangled and burned for his trouble at Vilvoorde in 1536. His dying prayer, "Lord, open the King of England's eyes," was answered within three years when Henry VIII authorized the Great Bible in 1539. Alexander Cruden in 1737 compiled the first great English concordance, working from a London garret, in and out of asylums for the rest of his eccentric life, but producing a tool that put every verse at the fingertips of every Bible reader. Strong's concordance of 1890 is the next step in that line. Each generation has chipped away at the wall between the ordinary Christian and the original Word of God. Strong's number system was the largest hammer of them all.

Our unit scripture is 2 Timothy 2:15. The English word "study" here translates the Greek spoudazô (Strong's G4704), which actually means "be diligent, give earnest effort." Paul is not telling Timothy to read more. He is telling Timothy to work hard at handling the Word. The phrase "rightly dividing" translates orthotomeô (Strong's G3718), "cut straight." Paul is telling Timothy to cut a straight line through Scripture like a stonemason cutting a stone or a tailor cutting cloth. Strong's numbers are the workman's tool. They do not make you a workman; they equip the workman you already want to be.

I. How Strong's Works: The Original Paper Concordance

I. Cómo funciona Strong: La concordancia original en papel

Imagine you have a hardcover copy of Strong's Exhaustive Concordance on your desk. (You will not have one in Mexico unless someone shipped it to you, but the structure is identical online and on your phone, so understanding the paper version teaches you how every digital tool works.) Open the concordance and you find three main parts:

PartWhat it contains
Main concordanceEvery English word in the KJV in alphabetical order. Under each word, every verse where it appears, with the Strong's number beside each occurrence.
Hebrew dictionary (back)Numbers H1 through H8674. Each number is one Hebrew word, with a brief definition, etymology, and list of how the KJV translates it.
Greek dictionary (back)Numbers G1 through G5624. Each number is one Greek word, with a brief definition, etymology, and list of how the KJV translates it.

The lookup process is four steps. Step one: find your verse in the main concordance under any meaningful word in it. Suppose you are studying John 3:16, "For God so loved the world …" Look up the word "loved." Step two: scan the list of every occurrence of "loved" in the KJV until you find John 3:16. Step three: note the Strong's number printed next to that occurrence. For "loved" in John 3:16 the number is G25. The "G" tells you Greek (NT); a "H" would tell you Hebrew (OT). Step four: flip to the Greek dictionary at the back, find entry G25, and read the lexical information: agapaô, the verb form of the noun agapê you learned in Unit 8. Self-giving sacrificial love. The same root that Christ used when He commanded us to love our enemies.

The whole process takes about ninety seconds once you know the rhythm. And the payoff is that you have just done what only seminary professors could do a hundred and fifty years ago. You have read John 3:16 in Greek without learning Greek grammar. Strong's number G25 was the bridge.

One warning, repeated

Strong's gives you a brief lexical entry — usually one or two sentences. It is a starting point, not the last word. Many serious Bible students go on to use larger lexicons like Thayer's (Greek) or Brown-Driver-Briggs (Hebrew). Both of these are keyed to Strong's numbers, so once you know how to find a Strong's number, you can look the same word up in any major lexicon. The number is the bridge. Once across the bridge, there is a whole library waiting.

(No tendrás una en México a menos que alguien te la haya enviado, pero la estructura es idéntica en línea y en tu teléfono, así que entender la versión en papel te enseña cómo funciona cada herramienta digital.) Supongamos que estás estudiando Juan 3:16, «Porque de tal manera amó Dios al mundo …» Busca la palabra «amó». Paso dos: recorre la lista de cada ocurrencia de «amó» en la KJV hasta que encuentres Juan 3:16. Paso tres: nota el número de Strong impreso junto a esa ocurrencia. Para «amó» en Juan 3:16 el número es G25. La «G» te dice griego (NT); una «H» te diría hebreo (AT). Paso cuatro: voltea al diccionario griego al final, encuentra la entrada G25, y lee la información léxica: agapaô, la forma verbal del sustantivo agapê que aprendiste en la Unidad 8. Amor sacrificial abnegado. La misma raíz que Cristo usó cuando nos mandó amar a nuestros enemigos.

Todo el proceso toma unos noventa segundos una vez que conoces el ritmo. Y la recompensa es que acabas de hacer lo que solo profesores de seminario podían hacer hace ciento cincuenta años. Has leído Juan 3:16 en griego sin aprender gramática griega. El número de Strong G25 fue el puente.

IV. Three Pitfalls Every Workman Must Avoid

IV. Tres trampas que todo obrero debe evitar

A power tool in the hand of an untrained workman is dangerous. Strong's numbers in the hand of a careless student have produced thousands of sincere but wrong sermons over the past century. Before we set you loose on Unit 11, you need to know the three classic mistakes and how to avoid them. None of them are problems with Strong's itself. All of them are problems with how the workman uses the tool.

Pitfall 1: The root fallacy. The assumption that the original meaning of a word's root is what the word "really" means in any later passage. Example: someone preaches "the church is dynamite!" from dynamis (Greek "power," from which we get the English word "dynamite") in Acts 1:8. But dynamis was used in Greek for five hundred years before dynamite was invented in 1867. The Greek word means "power" or "ability." It has nothing to do with explosives. The English derivative is fun trivia but bad exegesis. The rule: a word's meaning is determined by how it is used in its own period and context, not by where the word originally came from.
Pitfall 2: The illegitimate totality transfer. The assumption that a Greek or Hebrew word always means everything its lexicon entry lists, all at once, in every passage. Sôzô can mean rescue from drowning, rescue from disease, rescue from death, or rescue from sin (Unit 9 covered this). But in any single verse it usually means one of those things, not all four at once. The rule: the lexicon gives you the range of possible meanings; the context tells you which one the author intended in this verse.
Pitfall 3: The "deeper meaning" trap. The assumption that learning the Greek or Hebrew always reveals a "hidden" or "deeper" meaning that the English translation missed. Usually this is not true. Most modern translations are made by careful scholars who know far more Greek and Hebrew than any of us ever will. The English already captures 95% of what the Greek says. Word study deepens 5%, sharpens edges, and prevents misreadings — it does not normally overturn the meaning of the verse. The rule: Strong's enriches; Strong's does not normally correct your English Bible.

The Berean attitude (Acts 17:11)

When Paul preached at Berea, Luke records that the Bereans were "more noble … in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so." The Greek word for "searched" is anakrinô (Strong's G350), a courtroom verb meaning "to examine carefully, to investigate, to cross-examine." The Bereans loved Paul, but they did not take even his preaching at face value. They opened their Old Testament scrolls and checked his claims against the inspired text. This is the attitude that Strong's enables for every layperson. You no longer need to take any preacher's word for what the Greek says. You can anakrinô the claim. You can check it for yourself. Strong's numbers are the modern equivalent of the Berean's scroll.

Strong's makes every Christian a Berean. It hands the cross-examination tool to the layperson in the pew. A preacher who fears Strong's-equipped congregations is a preacher with something to hide.

Una herramienta poderosa en la mano de un obrero sin entrenamiento es peligrosa. Los números de Strong en la mano de un estudiante descuidado han producido miles de sermones sinceros pero erróneos durante el siglo pasado. Antes de soltarte en la Unidad 11, necesitas conocer los tres errores clásicos y cómo evitarlos. Ninguno de ellos es un problema con Strong en sí. Todos son problemas con cómo el obrero usa la herramienta.

Trampa 1: La falacia de la raíz. La suposición de que el significado original de la raíz de una palabra es lo que la palabra «realmente» significa en cualquier pasaje posterior. Ejemplo: alguien predica «¡la iglesia es dinamita!» desde dynamis (griego «poder», de donde obtenemos la palabra inglesa «dinamita») en Hechos 1:8. Pero dynamis se usó en griego por quinientos años antes de que la dinamita fuera inventada en 1867. La palabra griega significa «poder» o «habilidad». No tiene nada que ver con explosivos. El derivado en inglés es trivia divertida pero mala exegesis. La regla: el significado de una palabra está determinado por cómo se usa en su propio período y contexto, no por de dónde vino originalmente.
Trampa 2: La transferencia ilegítima de totalidad. La suposición de que una palabra griega o hebrea siempre significa todo lo que enumera su entrada en el lexicón, todo a la vez, en cada pasaje. Sôzô puede significar rescatar de ahogarse, rescatar de enfermedad, rescatar de muerte, o rescatar de pecado (la Unidad 9 cubrió esto). Pero en cualquier versículo individual usualmente significa una de esas cosas, no las cuatro a la vez. La regla: el lexicón te da el rango de significados posibles; el contexto te dice cuál intentaba el autor en este versículo.
Trampa 3: La trampa del «significado más profundo». La suposición de que aprender el griego o el hebreo siempre revela un significado «oculto» o «más profundo» que la traducción inglesa o castellana perdió. Usualmente esto no es verdad. La mayoría de las traducciones modernas son hechas por eruditos cuidadosos que saben más griego y hebreo que cualquiera de nosotros jamás sabrá. El español ya captura el 95% de lo que el griego dice. El estudio de palabras profundiza el 5%, afila los bordes, y previene malas lecturas — normalmente no revierte el significado del versículo. La regla: Strong enriquece; Strong normalmente no corrige tu Biblia.

La actitud bereana (Hechos 17:11)

Cuando Pablo predicó en Berea, Lucas registra que los bereanos eran «más nobles … pues recibieron la palabra con toda solicitud, escudriñando cada día las Escrituras para ver si estas cosas eran así». La palabra griega para «escudriñar» es anakrinô (número de Strong G350), un verbo de tribunal que significa «examinar cuidadosamente, investigar, interrogar». Los bereanos amaban a Pablo, pero no tomaron ni siquiera su predicación al pie de la letra. Abrieron sus rollos del Antiguo Testamento y verificaron sus afirmaciones contra el texto inspirado. Esta es la actitud que Strong habilita para cada laico. Ya no necesitas tomar la palabra de ningún predicador sobre lo que dice el griego. Puedes anakrinô la afirmación. Puedes verificarla por ti mismo. Los números de Strong son el equivalente moderno del rollo bereano.

Strong hace de cada cristiano un bereano. Pone la herramienta de interrogatorio en la mano del laico en la banca. Un predicador que teme a las congregaciones equipadas con Strong es un predicador con algo que esconder.

V. Putting It Together — and the Bridge to Unit 11

V. Poniendo todo junto — y el puente a la Unidad 11

You have now seen Strong's numbers in three forms. Paper for the historic foundation, phone for the daily workman, internet for the deep dive. All three contain the same essential content: every Hebrew and Greek word in the Bible keyed to a number, with a brief definition, and a list of every verse where the word appears. That single innovation — one number per word — broke down the wall between the seminary and the laity in 1890 and is still doing it in 2026.

What a complete Strong's word study looks like

Here is the workflow you will apply in Unit 11. Pick a verse. Pick a key word in that verse. Look up the Strong's number. Read the lexicon entry. Look up every other place the same Strong's number appears in Scripture. Read those other verses, noting how the same word is used in different contexts. Now go back to your original verse and reread it with all of that in your mind. You have just done a real word study. The whole process takes fifteen minutes in Blue Letter Bible. It used to take a seminary degree.
