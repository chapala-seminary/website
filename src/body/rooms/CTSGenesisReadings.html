Creation, the Image of God, and Human Vocation

Genesis Intensive — Genesis 1–2

1.Genesis begins with God, establishing the Creator-creature distinction

Genesis begins with God, establishing the Creator-creature distinction. Creation is good, ordered, and dependent upon Him; Christians may differ over some chronology and literary-form questions while affirming the authority of Scripture and God as Creator.

2.Male and female alike bear God’s image

Male and female alike bear God’s image. Human dignity therefore rests in God’s action rather than intelligence, usefulness, nationality, age, wealth, or power, and it remains morally significant after the Fall.

3.Dominion is delegated stewardship under God, not permission to exploit what belongs to Him

Dominion is delegated stewardship under God, not permission to exploit what belongs to Him. Humanity is called to cultivate creation with both creativity and creaturely restraint.

4.Work appears before the Fall

Work appears before the Fall. Sin makes labor painful and frustrated but does not make work itself a curse; ordinary vocation can therefore become faithful service to God and neighbor.

5.The New Testament’s renewal language moves toward restoration rather than contempt for creation

The New Testament’s renewal language moves toward restoration rather than contempt for creation. The image marred by sin is renewed in Christ, giving Christian anthropology both realism about sin and hope for transformed life.

Sin, Judgment, Grace, and the Nations

Genesis Intensive — Genesis 3–11

1.Genesis 3 presents temptation as an assault on trust in God’s word and goodness

Genesis 3 presents temptation as an assault on trust in God’s word and goodness. Sin is more than rule-breaking: the creature seeks moral autonomy, and shame, hiding, blame, and exile follow.

2.Genesis 3:21 explicitly says God made garments of skins; it does not explicitly describe an animal sacrifice

Genesis 3:21 explicitly says God made garments of skins; it does not explicitly describe an animal sacrifice. Later typological connections may be considered, but responsible interpretation distinguishes statement from inference.

3.Cain’s murder shows sin spreading into violence

Cain’s murder shows sin spreading into violence. God judges Cain while restraining unlimited vengeance, and cultural development alongside violence shows that progress alone cannot cure the human heart.

4.The Flood joins holy judgment with preserving grace

The Flood joins holy judgment with preserving grace. It should not be reduced to a modern geology examination; its theological force includes human violence, divine grief, judgment, preservation, and covenant mercy.

5.Babel depicts humanity seeking security and a name apart from God

Babel depicts humanity seeking security and a name apart from God. The text should not be forced into unsupported scientific claims about every language’s origin; its theological contrast appears when God next promises to make Abraham’s name great.

6.Babel flows into Abraham: the scattered nations remain in view when God promises blessing to all families of the earth

Babel flows into Abraham: the scattered nations remain in view when God promises blessing to all families of the earth. Pentecost may be seen as a qualified reversal because the gospel crosses linguistic barriers without erasing languages or nations.

Pecado, Juicio, Gracia y las Naciones

Génesis Intensivo — Genesis 3–11

1.Lectura 1

Génesis 3 presenta tentación como ataque contra confianza en palabra y bondad de Dios. Pecado es más que romper regla: criatura busca autonomía moral, y siguen vergüenza, escondimiento, culpa y exilio.

2.Lectura 2

Génesis 3:21 dice explícitamente que Dios hizo túnicas de pieles; no describe explícitamente sacrificio animal. Conexiones tipológicas posteriores pueden considerarse, pero interpretación responsable distingue declaración de inferencia.

3.Lectura 3

Asesinato de Caín muestra pecado extendiéndose a violencia. Dios juzga a Caín mientras restringe venganza ilimitada, y desarrollo cultural junto a violencia muestra que progreso solo no cura corazón humano.

4.Lectura 4

Diluvio une juicio santo con gracia preservadora. No debe reducirse a examen de geología moderna; su fuerza teológica incluye violencia humana, dolor divino, juicio, preservación y misericordia del pacto.

5.Lectura 5

Babel presenta humanidad buscando seguridad y nombre aparte de Dios. Texto no debe forzarse a afirmaciones científicas no sustentadas sobre origen de cada idioma; contraste teológico aparece cuando Dios promete después engrandecer nombre de Abraham.

José insiste que interpretaciones pertenecen a Dios y combina entendimiento revelado con planificación práctica para hambre. Su promoción sirve preservación de vida y no es fórmula de prosperidad para todo creyente fiel.

Disposición de Judá a ofrecerse por Benjamín demuestra cambio real. José perdona mientras nombra lo ocurrido; perdón puede coexistir con verdad, justicia, reconstrucción gradual de confianza, seguridad y límites sabios.
