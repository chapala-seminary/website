COURSE APPENDICES: GLOSSARY AND HISTORICAL TIMELINE

The glossary gives working definitions for this course. Some terms are contested; the entries identify the course’s usage and point to the relevant source notes. The timeline distinguishes a movement’s reported spiritual interpretation from documentary dates and organizational milestones.

## Glossary

**Aladura** — A Yoruba term commonly used for “praying people,” applied to several Nigerian and West African prayer and healing movements. It is an umbrella label, not one unified denomination. [U7.4]

**Azusa Street Revival** — The revival associated with William J. Seymour and the Apostolic Faith Mission at 312 Azusa Street in Los Angeles, beginning in 1906. Its influence spread through visitors, periodicals, and missionary networks; historians debate how it relates to other early revivals. [U3.4–U3.7]

**Baptism in the Holy Spirit** — In classical Pentecostal theology, an empowering experience of the Spirit distinct from conversion; many Trinitarian classical Pentecostal bodies teach that speaking in tongues is its initial physical evidence. Other Christians use the term differently. [U1.2; U4.6]

**Charismatic renewal** — Twentieth-century renewal movements in older Protestant, Anglican, and Roman Catholic churches that emphasized gifts such as tongues, healing, and prophecy while participants generally remained within their existing traditions. [U9.2; U9.5]

**Classical Pentecostalism** — Churches and networks historically connected to early twentieth-century Pentecostal revivals and their institutional descendants. The category includes Trinitarian and Oneness communities; this course presents a Trinitarian classical Pentecostal perspective. [U1.2; U4.7]

**Cessationism** — The theological view that some miraculous gifts, often including tongues and prophecy, ceased or are no longer ordinarily given after the apostolic era. Cessationist views vary; they should be represented through their own sources, not caricatured. [U1.6]

**Divine healing** — Prayer and theological expectation that God heals; Pentecostal traditions differ over how healing relates to the atonement, medical care, and the timing of healing. [U2.7; U10.5]

**Finished Work** — William H. Durham’s teaching that Christ’s work at Calvary provides the basis for the believer’s sanctification, challenging the Holiness-Pentecostal sequence of a separate second work of entire sanctification before Spirit baptism. [U4.3]

**Holiness movement** — A diverse nineteenth-century renewal movement, especially within Methodism, emphasizing entire sanctification, holy living, and a deeper work of grace. It shaped many early Pentecostal leaders but did not itself constitute Pentecostalism. [U2.4–U2.5]

**Initial evidence** — The classical Pentecostal teaching that speaking in tongues is the first outward sign accompanying baptism in the Holy Spirit. It is a doctrinal interpretation of biblical narratives, not a description accepted by all Christian traditions. [U3.1–U3.3; U4.6]

**Mukti revival** — The 1905–1907 revival reported at Pandita Ramabai’s Mukti mission in India, with prayer and spiritual gifts. Minnie Abrams interpreted it through emerging Pentecostal language; do not assume Ramabai used precisely the same theological categories. [U3.9; U8.1]

**Neo-charismatic / independent Pentecostal** — A broad, varied category for independent churches and networks emphasizing charismatic gifts and spiritual power but not necessarily descended from classical Pentecostal denominations or sharing their doctrines. Usage varies among scholars. [U1.2–U1.3]

**Oneness Pentecostalism** — A Pentecostal theological family that rejects the classical Trinitarian distinction of three divine persons and teaches that the one God is revealed in Jesus Christ. Trinitarian Pentecostals disagree with this account of God; describe it accurately before stating that disagreement. [U4.7; U8.6]

**Pentecostal** — A contested umbrella term. Narrow definitions center on Spirit baptism and tongues; broader definitions include overlapping emphases such as healing, experiential worship, prophecy, and expectation of the Spirit’s activity. [U1.2–U1.3]

**Prosperity teaching / prosperity gospel** — A family of teachings linking faith, giving, positive confession, or spiritual practice with health and material well-being. Its forms differ. This course’s classical Pentecostal critique rejects promises that make wealth a guaranteed reward or blame sufferers for illness or poverty. [U10.3–U10.5]

**Third Wave** — A term associated with C. Peter Wagner for a late twentieth-century movement emphasizing signs and wonders in churches not necessarily identified as classical Pentecostal or charismatic. It is a useful but contested label. [U9.1; U9.6]

**Trinitarian** — Confessing one God as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. In this course, “Trinitarian classical Pentecostal” identifies the confessional standpoint of the lessons. [U1.1; U4.6]

**World Christian Database estimate** — A researched estimate using defined categories and demographic methods. The 2025 figure used here combines Pentecostals and Charismatics; it is not an audited membership count of classical Pentecostal denominations. [U1.4; U10.1]

## Historical Timeline

| Date | Event | Historical note |
|---|---|---|
| 1738 | John Wesley reported an experience of assurance at Aldersgate in London. | A formative Methodist event; not a Pentecostal event. [U2.1] |
| Mid-1830s | Sarah Lankford began Tuesday holiness meetings; Phoebe Palmer later led them. | Sources differ on the first meeting date and on which stage is called the meeting’s founding. [U2.4] |
| 1867 | National Holiness camp meetings developed into an organized association. | A key institutional milestone in the Holiness movement. [U2.5] |
| 1875 | First Keswick Convention met in England. | Associated with T. D. Harford-Battersby and Robert Wilson. [U2.6] |
| 1901 | Topeka meetings reported tongues; Agnes Ozman is identified in the usual account as the first student to speak in tongues there. | Early accounts differ in chronology and interpretation; Parham’s teaching became influential. [U3.1–U3.3] |
| 1904–1905 | Welsh Revival associated with Evan Roberts. | A revival that influenced some Pentecostal expectations; its direct relationship to classical Pentecostal origins is debated. [U3.8] |
| 1905–1907 | Revival at the Mukti mission in India. | Minnie Abrams’s 1906 account is a primary source; Ramabai’s own interpretation should not be conflated with Abrams’s. [U3.9] |
| 1906 | Azusa Street meetings began in Los Angeles under William J. Seymour; *The Apostolic Faith* began publication. | Strong documentary evidence of the mission’s activity and influence, while claims about its place as a singular origin are debated. [U3.4–U3.7] |
| 1909–1910 | Chilean Methodist revival in Valparaiso; church foundation and separation dates vary by source and refer to related stages. | Chile’s National Library dates the Iglesia Metodista Pentecostal’s founding to September 12, 1909; other accounts stress the 1910 break from Methodist oversight. [U3.10; U6.1] |
| 1910–1911 | Early Brazilian Pentecostal work: Francescon among Italian-speaking communities; Berg and Vingren arrived in Belém in 1910 and a congregation followed in 1911. | Assemblies of God naming followed in 1918. [U6.2–U6.3] |
| 1913–1916 | Oneness controversy and Assemblies of God doctrinal response. | Assemblies of God organized in 1914 and adopted its Statement of Fundamental Truths in 1916. [U4.4–U4.6] |
| 1920s–1935 | K. E. Abraham and co-workers formed a South Indian Pentecostal fellowship; the IPC name was adopted in 1934 and registration followed in 1935. | IPC sources differ over early convention dates; this chronology avoids asserting one exact founding date. [U8.2] |
| 1921 | Jing Dianying began a precursor Christian association in Shandong that developed into the Jesus Family. | Scholarly accounts describe the Jesus Family itself as emerging during the 1920s; its communal organization ended by the early 1950s. [U8.7] |
| 1921–1926 | Pentecostal missions are documented in the Philippines; the Assemblies of God missionary arrived in 1926. | A dated Pew historical overview distinguishes these milestones. [U8.9] |
| 1947 | An international Pentecostal gathering met in Zurich. | Later Pentecostal networks formalized separately; “conference” and “fellowship” are not interchangeable. [U10.6] |
| 1960 | Dennis Bennett publicly announced his Pentecostal experience at St. Mark’s Episcopal Church in Van Nuys. | Often used as a marker for the wider Charismatic renewal. [U9.2–U9.3] |
| 1965 | A major revival associated with Timor took place amid wider Indonesian awakenings and political upheaval. | The event and its interpretation are debated; the cited historical study weighs multiple causes and the limits of reports. [U8.8] |
| 1972 | International Pentecostal–Catholic dialogue began. | Dialogue is evidence of formal conversation, not doctrinal agreement. [U9.5; U10.7] |
| c. 1983–1988 | “Third Wave” became a label associated with C. Peter Wagner. | The period and scope vary by author; use the term as a model, not a fixed institutional movement. [U9.1; U9.6] |
| 1994 | Memphis reconciliation gathering addressed racial division among Pentecostal leaders. | Remembered as a significant act of repentance and reconciliation; it did not erase all continuing inequities. [U5.6] |
| 2010 | Lausanne Movement issued its Statement on the Prosperity Gospel and the Cape Town Commitment. | These documents acknowledge the appeal of prosperity teaching and criticize its distortions and harms. [U10.5] |
| 2025 | Global estimate lists 663,863,000 Pentecostals/Charismatics. | Broad category estimate, not classical denominational membership. [U1.4; U10.1] |

## Citation and Historical Scope Note

This course includes keyed references in the lessons, source notes at the end of each unit, and citations for its glossary and timeline. Full bibliographic details and page ranges are supplied where they were verified from the source record. Dates, totals, classifications, and causal explanations are qualified where reliable sources differ or the evidence is limited. The course presents its classical Pentecostal convictions as confessional teaching and identifies contested historical interpretations with their sources.
