# 1 Peter Intensive: Proposed Additions for Dr. Cook's Review

## Overall assessment

The course is warm, pastoral, and faithful to the text. It keeps Peter's main theme, exile identity leading to endurance in suffering, in view from Unit 1 to Unit 12, and its illustrations are memorable. It handles the hard texts (foreknowledge in 1:2, sinlessness of Christ, spirits in prison, baptism in 3:21) fairly and without pushing one side. The biggest gaps are background matters a pastor will be asked about: who actually wrote the letter and what part Silvanus played; what "Babylon" and "Mark my son" mean; the fallen-angels reading of 3:19, which the unit lists but never explains; what kind of persecution the readers were actually facing; and the cultural setting of the household instructions in chapter 3. A few illustrations and historical details also need correcting (see the list at the end).

## Proposals

### 1. Unit 1, after [b014]: Who Wrote the Letter, and How?

**WHY:** Students will meet the modern claim that a Galilean fisherman could not have written such polished Greek. The course never answers it, and the answer (Silvanus, 5:12) is right there in the letter.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

Who wrote the letter, and how? The letter names its author plainly: "Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 1:1), who calls himself "a witness of the sufferings of Christ" (1 Peter 5:1). The early church accepted this without hesitation. Second Peter speaks of "this second epistle" (2 Peter 3:1), pointing back to this one. Polycarp, who died about A.D. 155, quotes 1 Peter often, and by about A.D. 180 Irenaeus cites it by Peter's name.

Some modern scholars still question Peter's authorship, mainly because the Greek of the letter is careful and smooth, and it quotes the Greek Old Testament with ease. Could a fisherman whom the council called "uneducated and untrained" (Acts 4:13) write like this? There are good answers. First, the council meant Peter had no formal training as a rabbi, not that he could not read or write. Greek was spoken widely in Galilee, and Peter had preached for some thirty years by this time. Second, Peter tells us he had help: "By Silvanus, our faithful brother as I consider him, I have written to you briefly" (1 Peter 5:12). Silvanus is the Silas of Acts, a leader and prophet of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:22, 32), a Roman citizen (Acts 16:37), and Paul's partner in writing to the Thessalonians (1 Thessalonians 1:1). Writers in that world often used a trained secretary; Paul did, as Romans 16:22 shows: "I, Tertius, who wrote this epistle, greet you in the Lord." The phrase "by Silvanus" may mean that Silvanus helped put Peter's message into polished Greek, or that he carried the letter to the churches (compare Acts 15:23, "They wrote this letter by them"), or both.

The most reasonable conclusion is the one the church has always held: the letter is truly Peter's, and the apostle may have used a gifted brother to help him write it. There is a lesson in that too. Even an apostle did not do the Lord's work alone.

### 2. Unit 7, after [b012]: A Wife Who Chose Christ on Her Own

**WHY:** The cultural background shows how bold Peter's words to wives actually were. It also lets the course mention the main evangelical views on marriage roles fairly, and it adds a needed pastoral safeguard about abuse, all without changing anything the exams test (voluntary submission, winning the husband, equal heirs).

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

A word of background helps us see how bold Peter's counsel was. In the Greek and Roman world a wife was expected to follow her husband's religion. The writer Plutarch, who lived around Peter's time, advised that a wife should worship only her husband's gods and "shut the door" to strange religions. So a woman who became a Christian while her husband stayed a pagan had already done something her society considered disloyal. Peter does not tell her to give up Christ to keep the peace. He treats her as a person who has made her own choice before God, and he shows her how to live it out wisely, so that her husband may be won "without a word." Her submission is real, but it never includes giving up her Lord. That is why Peter can add that she should do good "and not be afraid with any terror" (1 Peter 3:6).

Sincere evangelical Christians apply this passage to marriage today in more than one way. Some hold that a husband's loving, servant leadership in the home is a lasting pattern God has given. Others stress that Peter also calls all believers to "be submissive to one another" (1 Peter 5:5; compare Ephesians 5:21), and they read his counsel to wives mainly as a missionary strategy for that culture. Many couples simply see marriage as a team in which husband and wife share decisions under Christ. CTS sets these views before you so you can study them yourself. All of them agree on what Peter makes plain: husband and wife are "heirs together of the grace of life" (1 Peter 3:7), and a husband who mistreats his wife cannot be right with God.

One pastoral caution has to be stated clearly. Biblical submission never means a wife must stay in danger. When there is violence or abuse, the church's duty is to protect the one at risk, and nothing in this passage teaches otherwise.

### 3. Unit 9, after [b017]: Why Many Read the "Spirits" as Fallen Angels

**WHY:** The second view (fallen angels) gets only one sentence, yet it is the view many recent evangelical commentators prefer, and its Old Testament and Jewish background is the key that makes the passage readable. This also gives a needed safeguard: none of these views teaches a second chance after death.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

It helps to understand why the second view has persuaded many careful readers, especially in recent decades. In the New Testament the word "spirits," used by itself, usually refers to spiritual beings rather than to the souls of dead people. Genesis 6:1-4 tells of "the sons of God" in the days just before the flood, and many Jews in Peter's time understood these to be angels who rebelled. A well-known Jewish book of that era, called 1 Enoch, told of these rebel angels being imprisoned and of judgment being announced to them. That book is not Scripture, but Jude quotes from it (Jude 14-15), so the readers of the New Testament knew it. Peter's own second letter makes the same connection between the imprisoned angels and Noah: "For if God did not spare the angels who sinned, but cast them down to hell and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved for judgment; and did not spare the ancient world, but saved Noah, one of eight people, a preacher of righteousness..." (2 Peter 2:4-5; compare Jude 6). On this reading, the word "preached" means "proclaimed" or "announced." The risen Christ declared His victory to the rebel powers, which fits the passage's closing words, "angels and authorities and powers having been made subject to Him" (1 Peter 3:22).

Whichever view a student holds, one thing should be stated plainly: this passage does not teach that the lost receive a second chance to be saved after death. Scripture is clear that "it is appointed for men to die once, but after this the judgment" (Hebrews 9:27). Some readers connect this passage with 1 Peter 4:6, "the gospel was preached also to those who are dead." As Unit 10 explains, that verse is best understood as speaking of believers who heard the gospel while they were alive and have since died. The urgent call of the gospel is for today.

### 4. Unit 11, after [b017]: What Kind of Suffering Were They Facing, and What Does It Mean for Us?

**WHY:** The course says the letter was written near the time of Nero, but it never explains that the readers' suffering was mostly social (slander, rejection, family conflict) rather than an official empire-wide persecution. That is exactly what many CTS students in Latin America and Africa face, and they need practical guidance for it.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

What kind of suffering were these believers facing? Read the letter closely and you will see that it was mostly social. They were spoken against as evildoers (1 Peter 2:12; 3:16). Old friends thought it strange that they no longer joined in pagan feasts, and they slandered them for it (1 Peter 4:4). Wives lived with unbelieving husbands, and household servants could be beaten by harsh masters. The very name "Christian," which appears only three times in the New Testament (Acts 11:26; 26:28; 1 Peter 4:16), was used as an insult. In Rome, under Nero, blood was being shed. But for most of Peter's readers in Asia Minor, the fire was mockery, rejection, lost business, and broken family ties. Peter treats this as real suffering, and so should we.

Many believers today know the same fire. A new believer may be rejected by his family for leaving the religion of his parents. A village may shut out the family that will not take part in its festivals or rituals. In some regions pastors who stand against crime or corruption receive threats. Peter's letter gives such believers clear counsel:

- **Make sure the suffering is for Christ.** Peter warns against suffering "as a busybody" (1 Peter 4:15). Suffering because of harshness, foolishness, or political quarrels brings no honor to Christ.
- **Do not be surprised.** This is the normal road for those who follow a crucified Lord (1 Peter 4:12).
- **Be family to the rejected.** Peter's call to "be hospitable to one another without grumbling" (1 Peter 4:9) means much to a believer whose own family has closed the door.
- **Return blessing for insult** (1 Peter 3:9), and keep on doing good (1 Peter 4:19).
- **Use godly wisdom.** Accepting suffering does not mean seeking it out. The Lord Himself said, "When they persecute you in this city, flee to another" (Matthew 10:23). Leaving a place of danger is not a lack of faith.

### 5. Unit 12, after [b027]: "Babylon" and "Mark My Son"

**WHY:** [b014] states the majority view of "Babylon" in one sentence. Here at the letter's end the major views can be set out fairly, and the course can draw out a beautiful pastoral point that is easy to miss: the letter closes with two men the Lord restored after failure.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

Two names in the final greeting are worth a closer look. First, "Babylon." Christians have understood it in three main ways. A few take it literally, as the old city on the Euphrates River, where a Jewish community still lived, though no early tradition places Peter there. A few others point to a Roman fort called Babylon in Egypt, near present-day Cairo. Most interpreters, from the early church until today, understand it as a code name for Rome, as it seems to be in Revelation (Revelation 14:8; 17:5). Early tradition says Peter ministered in Rome and died there under Nero. The name also fits the message of the whole letter. Peter's readers are "pilgrims of the Dispersion," and Peter writes to them from "Babylon," the city of exile. Writer and readers alike are God's people living far from home. "She who is in Babylon, elect together with you" is most naturally taken as the church in that city, though some have suggested it means Peter's wife, who traveled with him (1 Corinthians 9:5).

Second, "Mark my son." This is John Mark, a spiritual son to Peter rather than a son by birth. Years earlier Mark had abandoned Paul and Barnabas on their first missionary journey (Acts 13:13), and Paul refused to take him on the next one (Acts 15:37-38). Yet Mark was restored, and near the end of his life Paul wrote, "Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11). An early church leader named Papias, writing in the early 100s, reports that Mark's Gospel records the preaching of Peter.

So the letter ends with two men side by side: Peter, who denied his Lord three times, and Mark, who turned back from the mission field. Both failed, and both were restored and used by God. For every reader who has stumbled, that closing greeting is itself a word of hope: failure need not be the end of your service.

## Errors or problems found

1. **[b016], Unit 6: not the NKJV wording.** "Take this cup from Me. Nevertheless, not My will, but Thine, be done" mixes wordings. NKJV Luke 22:42 reads: "Father, if it is Your will, take this cup away from Me; nevertheless not My will, but Yours, be done."
2. **[b012], Unit 6: the slave figure is doubtful.** The claim of "some sixty million slaves" comes from 19th-century popular writing. Historians estimate the whole Roman Empire had roughly 50–60 million people, with perhaps 10–20% enslaved. Suggested wording: "many millions of slaves."
3. **[b025], Unit 6: the George Wilson story is overstated.** The case is real (United States v. Wilson, 1833, Chief Justice John Marshall), but the records do not clearly show that Wilson was hanged, and the quoted words are a paraphrase, not Marshall's actual ruling. Suggested wording: "According to the story as it is often told..."
4. **[b019], Unit 9: the Gehenna "garbage dump" is unproven.** No ancient source describes the Valley of Hinnom as a burning garbage dump. That idea first appears around A.D. 1200 (Rabbi David Kimhi). The Bible remembers the valley as the place of child sacrifice to Molech (2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 7:31). If an exam question relies on "garbage dump," soften the wording to "tradition describes it as..." instead of removing it.
5. **[b017], Unit 9: the claim about which views are most held is questionable.** The statement that views three and four "are the most widely held among evangelical Christians today" is doubtful. The release-of-Old-Testament-saints view is older and less common among evangelical scholars now, and many recent evangelical commentators favor the fallen-angels view. Consider: "Each of these views has able evangelical defenders." Also in [b016], add references for "a preacher of righteousness" (2 Peter 2:5) and "leading captivity captive" (Ephesians 4:8-9).
6. **[b021], Unit 10: C. T. Studd details.** He gave away most of his inheritance soon after receiving it (1887). The remaining money was given at the time of his marriage in 1888, when he gave it to his bride, who passed it to General Booth. That was decades before his death, not "near the end of his life." Also, "the finest cricket player in all of England" overstates it; say "one of England's finest cricketers."
7. **[b019]–[b021], Unit 8: textual note.** The heading reads "Sanctify Christ as Lord," but the NKJV text reads "the Lord God." One sentence would prevent confusion, for example: "Most older Greek manuscripts read 'Christ as Lord'; either way, Peter means the same Lord Jesus."
8. **[b013], Unit 1: small detail.** In John 21 the three charges are "Feed My lambs," "Tend My sheep," and "Feed My sheep," not "Feed My sheep" three times. Suggested wording: "three times charging him to feed and tend His flock."
9. **Repetition (not an error).** Several units have back-to-back paragraphs that say nearly the same thing: Unit 1 [b016]/[b017] and [b024]/[b026]; Unit 4 [b014]/[b015] and [b022]/[b023]; Unit 5 [b011]/[b012]; Unit 7 [b020]/[b021]. If the exams do not depend on the second paragraph, trimming would make room for the additions above without growing the units.
10. **Illustration balance, Unit 3.** The cat and sow, the train, the sage at the river, the umbrella factory, and the dentist ([b019], [b020], [b022], [b024], [b025]) together come close to or just over the 20% guideline.
