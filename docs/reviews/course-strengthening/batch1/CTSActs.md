# Acts Intensive — Proposed Additions for Dr. Cook's Approval

## Overall assessment

This is a warm, preachable course. The narrative moves well, it follows Acts 1:8 clearly, and it is full of memorable lessons for pastors (Barnabas, John Mark, Aquila and Priscilla, "be of good cheer"). It already treats two debated texts fairly: Acts 13:48 and Lydia in Acts 16:14 both get balanced Reformed/Arminian notes. The biggest gap is that the texts our Pentecostal and non-Pentecostal students argue about most, Acts 2:38 and the Spirit "receivings" in Acts 2, 8, 10 and 19, get no balanced treatment. Unit 8 in fact takes one side (the twelve at Ephesus "were still lost"). The course also never teaches how to tell what Acts *describes* from what it *commands*, and it says little about the Roman/Jewish world of Paul's journeys or about his church-planting method, which is the most practical subject for our students. One caution on length: Units 5–10 already run 3,200–3,500 words, and Unit 10 is about 4,800. So none of the proposals below go into Unit 10. If you adopt several of them, you might shorten an illustration elsewhere to make room (for example the bricklayer letter in Unit 10 [b036]).

## Proposal 1 — Unit 1, after [b031]
**Title:** "For the Remission of Sins": How Christians Read Acts 2:38

**WHY:** Acts 2:38 is one of the most argued-over verses among our students' churches, Oneness and Trinitarian Pentecostals among them. Right now the course quotes it without comment.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> Before we leave Peter's answer, we need to slow down, because few verses in the Bible have caused more discussion among sincere Christians than Acts 2:38. Two questions come up again and again.
>
> The first question is about the words "baptized … for the remission of sins." Many evangelical Christians, especially those who practice believer's baptism, understand Peter to mean that baptism is the public act of a person who has repented and been forgiven. It is the outward sign of an inward change. They point out that Luke often connects forgiveness to repentance and faith without mentioning baptism: "Repent therefore and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out" (Acts 3:19, NKJV); "whoever believes in Him will receive remission of sins" (Acts 10:43, NKJV). They also note that Cornelius's household received the Holy Spirit *before* they were baptized (Acts 10:47). Other Christians believe God actually gives forgiveness through baptism when it is received in faith. They point to Ananias's words to Paul: "Arise and be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on the name of the Lord" (Acts 22:16, NKJV). Churches that baptize infants often connect Acts 2:39, "the promise is to you and to your children," with the household baptisms in Acts 16. What all these readings share is that Peter never separates baptism from repentance and faith in Jesus Christ.
>
> The second question is about the phrase "in the name of Jesus Christ." Most churches, including most Pentecostal churches, understand it to mean "by the authority of Jesus" and "into belonging to Jesus." They baptize with the words the Lord Himself gave: "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19, NKJV). Oneness churches read Acts 2:38 as the required spoken formula. Trinitarian Christians answer that Luke uses several phrases ("in the name of the Lord Jesus," "in the name of the Lord") and that he is describing *whose* baptism it is, not giving a script.
>
> A pastor should know these views, be able to explain them kindly, and teach his own convictions from the whole of Scripture, not from one verse alone.

## Proposal 2 — Unit 6, after [b017]
**Title:** The World the Missionaries Walked Into

**WHY:** The course has a map, but it never explains the background that makes Paul's journeys make sense: Roman roads and peace, the Greek language, Jewish synagogues across the Empire, and the "God-fearers." Students will understand Acts 13–28 much better with this background.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> Before Barnabas and Saul set sail, it helps to see the world God had prepared for them. Paul would later write, "But when the fullness of the time had come, God sent forth His Son" (Galatians 4:4, NKJV). By the middle of the first century, God had set the stage in at least four ways.
>
> First, there was Roman order. For the first time in history one government ruled the whole Mediterranean world. Piracy had been largely cleared from the seas. Roman roads, paved with stone and marked with milestones, ran from city to city. The Egnatian Way, which Paul will walk in Macedonia, and the Appian Way into Rome were highways of empire that became highways of the gospel. A missionary could travel farther and more safely than ever before.
>
> Second, there was a common language. Thanks to Alexander the Great, ordinary people from Syria to Italy spoke everyday Greek alongside their local tongues. Paul could preach in Lystra, Athens and Rome without an interpreter, and the New Testament itself would be written in that Greek.
>
> Third, there were synagogues everywhere. Several million Jews lived outside the land of Israel, in what is called the Dispersion. Almost every city had a synagogue where the Old Testament was read every Sabbath. That is why Paul's custom was to go "to the Jew first." The synagogue gave him an audience that already knew the one true God and the promises of a Messiah.
>
> Fourth, there were the God-fearers. Many Gentiles, tired of the empty idols of their cities, attended the synagogue and worshiped Israel's God without becoming full converts to Judaism. Cornelius was one; Lydia was another. Again and again in Acts these God-fearers were the bridge across which the gospel walked into the Gentile world.
>
> None of this was an accident. The same Lord who sends the missionary prepares the road ahead of him. He still does it today, through radio, the internet, migration and common languages, if we have eyes to see the roads He has built.

## Proposal 3 — Unit 6, after [b037]
**Title:** How the Council Decided, and What It Means for Church Planters Today

**WHY:** Unit 6 tells what the council decided, but not how the church came to its decision (testimony, Scripture, the Spirit, consensus). It also does not ask what Acts 15 means for cross-cultural mission today, which is one of the most practical questions our students face.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> Notice how the council reached its decision, because the method is as instructive as the result. First, they listened to testimony: Peter told what God had done at Cornelius's house, and Paul and Barnabas told what God had done among the Gentiles. Second, they tested that experience by Scripture: James quoted Amos, "So that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord, even all the Gentiles who are called by My name" (Acts 15:17, NKJV). Third, they sought the mind of the Spirit together. Their letter says, "For it seemed good to the Holy Spirit, and to us, to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things" (Acts 15:28, NKJV). Experience, Scripture, the Spirit, and the agreement of the church. That is a good pattern for any church facing a hard question.
>
> Bible students have understood the four requests in different ways. Many see them mainly as matters of love and table fellowship: Jewish believers were deeply offended by these things, "for Moses has had throughout many generations those who preach him in every city" (Acts 15:21, NKJV). Others note that the list closely follows the laws for foreigners living in Israel in Leviticus 17–18. Most agree that the call to flee sexual immorality and idolatry remains for all Christians, while the food requests were tied to that first-century setting.
>
> Now bring this home to the mission field. Every church planter carries his own culture in his suitcase. The question of Acts 15 is still with us: what must a new believer give up, and what may he keep? The council's answer is clear. Salvation is by grace through faith alone, and we must not add our customs to the gospel. We must not require a new believer to adopt our clothing, our music, our language, or our style of worship. We must call every believer away from idols and immorality. And we should ask all believers to limit their freedom out of love for one another. A church planter in a village in Africa or a barrio in Mexico who learns that difference will plant churches that belong to Christ and feel at home among their own people.

## Proposal 4 — Unit 7, after [b041]
**Title:** Paul's Church-Planting Pattern

**WHY:** The course tells the story of the journeys well but never pulls together Paul's method. For pastors and lay leaders who plant churches today, this is some of the most useful material in Acts.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> Step back from the second journey and look at the pattern. Paul did not wander at random. Several habits show up in city after city, and church planters today can learn from every one of them.
>
> He went to strategic cities. Philippi, Thessalonica, Corinth and later Ephesus sat on the great roads and sea lanes. A church planted there could spread the word to the countryside. Within months Paul could write to the Thessalonians, "For from you the word of the Lord has sounded forth, not only in Macedonia and Achaia, but also in every place" (1 Thessalonians 1:8, NKJV).
>
> He started with people already prepared. "Then Paul, as his custom was, went in to them, and for three Sabbaths reasoned with them from the Scriptures" (Acts 17:2, NKJV). He began with the synagogue and the God-fearers, then moved outward.
>
> He built on households. Lydia's house, the jailer's house, Jason's house, the house of Justus and of Crispus. The early church had no buildings; it had homes. Most churches in our regions still begin the same way.
>
> He worked as a team. Silas, Timothy, Luke, Aquila and Priscilla. Paul almost never ministered alone, and he was always training the people traveling with him.
>
> He supported himself when necessary, so that the gospel would never look like a business.
>
> He handed leadership to local believers quickly. Only weeks or months after planting churches in Galatia, "they had appointed elders in every church, and prayed with fasting, they commended them to the Lord in whom they had believed" (Acts 14:23, NKJV). He trusted the Holy Spirit to keep working in young leaders.
>
> He followed up. He went back to visit, sent co-workers, and wrote letters, several of which are now in our New Testament.
>
> You will see this pattern reach its peak in the next unit, in the lecture hall of Tyrannus at Ephesus, where training disciples spread the word through a whole province. Paul's goal was never one church. It was churches that would plant churches: "commit these to faithful men who will be able to teach others also" (2 Timothy 2:2, NKJV).

## Proposal 5 — Unit 8, after [b022]
**Title:** Four Times the Spirit Came: Reading Acts 2, 8, 10 and 19 Fairly

**WHY:** Many CTS students are Pentecostal or charismatic and many are not. Right now the course reads Acts 19 one way only (b020–b021, b046), and it never brings the four key texts together. This addition lays out the main readings without deciding for the student. It leaves the lecture's own reading standing as one of them, so the exam is not contradicted.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> We should pause here, because Acts 19 is one of four passages that Christians study together and understand differently. In Acts 2 the believers in Jerusalem were filled with the Spirit and spoke in tongues. In Acts 8 the Samaritans believed and were baptized, yet "as yet He had fallen upon none of them" (Acts 8:16, NKJV) until Peter and John laid hands on them. In Acts 10 the Spirit fell on Cornelius's household while Peter was still preaching, before baptism, and they spoke in tongues. In Acts 19 the twelve at Ephesus received the Spirit when Paul laid hands on them, and they spoke in tongues and prophesied.
>
> Classical Pentecostals see in these passages a pattern: the baptism in the Holy Spirit is an experience of empowering for witness that can come after salvation, with speaking in tongues as its initial sign. They note that the Samaritans had truly believed, and that Luke calls the Ephesians "disciples."
>
> Many charismatic Christians agree that believers can and should seek fresh fillings and spiritual gifts after conversion. They do not usually require tongues as the necessary sign.
>
> Many non-Pentecostal evangelicals read these events as special moments when the gospel crossed a new frontier: Jews, Samaritans, Gentiles, and followers of John. God gave visible signs so that the church would know these groups belonged fully to the one body. They emphasize, "For by one Spirit we were all baptized into one body" (1 Corinthians 12:13, NKJV), and they read the twelve at Ephesus, as this lecture has, as men who had not yet come to full faith in Christ.
>
> All three groups agree on a great deal. Every true believer has the Holy Spirit. Every believer is commanded, "be filled with the Spirit" (Ephesians 5:18, NKJV). And the Spirit's power is given for witness. Learn these readings well, so you can explain your own conviction from the text and still embrace brothers and sisters who read it differently.

## Proposal 6 — Unit 11, after [b029]
**Title:** Reading Acts Wisely: What Luke Describes and What God Commands

**WHY:** The course calls students to "live Acts 29," but it never gives them the basic interpretive tool for doing that: telling the difference between what Acts *describes* and what it *prescribes*. Without that tool, readers may treat every event as a command (casting lots, selling property, handkerchief healings) or dismiss Acts as only history.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> Before we talk about living Acts 29, we need one tool for reading Acts well. Acts is history, true history inspired by the Holy Spirit, and "all Scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine" (2 Timothy 3:16, NKJV). But not everything Luke *describes* is something God *commands* every church to repeat. Bible teachers call this the difference between descriptive and prescriptive passages.
>
> Think of some examples. The apostles chose Matthias by casting lots (Acts 1:26). No church today is told to choose its pastors that way. The Jerusalem believers "had all things in common" (Acts 4:32, NKJV), yet Peter told Ananias that his property was his own (Acts 5:4), and the later churches did not all sell their land. Handkerchiefs from Paul's body brought healing (Acts 19:12), and Luke calls those miracles "unusual." We should not turn them into a method.
>
> So how do we know what is meant for us? Here are four simple questions. First, does Luke repeat it again and again as the normal pattern? Bold preaching of Christ, prayer, repentance and faith, baptism of believers, care for the poor, and the Spirit-empowered witness of ordinary believers appear on almost every page. Second, does Luke clearly present it as good or as bad? Ananias and Sapphira are a warning, not a model. Third, does the rest of the New Testament, especially the letters, teach it directly? Fourth, is it tied to a once-only moment in salvation history, like Pentecost itself or the apostles as eyewitnesses of the resurrection?
>
> Sincere Christians apply these questions differently, especially to the gifts of the Spirit, as we saw in Unit 8. But the tool protects us from two errors: copying every detail of Acts as law, and treating Acts as an old story with nothing to say to us. Read Acts as Luke meant it to be read, as the pattern of a Spirit-led, Christ-preaching, mission-hearted church. Then go and write the next chapter.

## Errors or problems found

1. **Unit 10 [b015], factual error.** "his brother had been Nero's tutor." Felix's brother was Pallas, a powerful freedman and financial minister under Claudius. Nero's tutor was Seneca. Suggested fix: "his brother Pallas was one of the most powerful freedmen at the emperor's court."
2. **Unit 11 [b025], arithmetic error.** Perlman's 1995 concert was about "nineteen and a half centuries" after Philippi (c. AD 50), not "eighteen and a half."
3. **Unit 8 [b019]–[b021], [b046], guardrail conflict.** The lecture states flatly that the twelve Ephesian disciples "had not yet heard the gospel" and "were still lost." That is the non-Pentecostal reading presented as settled fact. Proposal 5 balances it without changing the existing text. You may also want to soften "They were still lost" to "Many believe they were still lost."
4. **Unit 1 [b028], minor factual error.** The "first sheaf" (barley) was offered during Passover week (Leviticus 23:10–11). At Pentecost the offering was two loaves of bread from the new wheat (Leviticus 23:17). Suggested fix: "brought two loaves made from the first of the wheat harvest."
5. **Unit 10 [b041], minor sequence issue.** Paul's "take heart" speech (Acts 27:21–26) comes *before* "the fourteenth night" (27:27), so "Paul stood up among them on the fourteenth night" should read "Late in the storm, Paul stood up among them." (The breakfast speech of 27:33–34 is the one that happens on the fourteenth night.)
6. **Unit 10 [b020], minor.** Drusilla was in her mid-to-late teens when she married Felix, but she would have been about twenty by Paul's hearing (c. AD 57–58). Suggested fix: "had been married to Felix as a teenager."
7. **Unit 10 [b025], minor.** The right of appeal to *Caesar* dates from Augustus, not "the founding of the republic." (The older republican right was appeal to the people.) Suggested fix: "had been part of Roman law since the days of Augustus."
8. **Unit 10 [b030], voice inconsistency.** "One of Dr. Cook's older Acts 26 sermons…" refers to the professor in the third person in the middle of a first-person lecture. Suggested fix: "One of my older sermons on Acts 26…"
9. **Unit 9 [b049], minor.** Herod Agrippa I was struck down in the theater at Caesarea, not in "Herod's palace." Suggested fix: "in the same city where Herod Agrippa I had been struck down."
10. **Unit 9 [b016], minor.** Acts 18:18 says Paul "had taken a vow" at Cenchrea. It does not say "Nazirite vows" (plural). Suggested fix: "had taken a vow at Cenchrea."
11. **Unit 11 [b022], minor.** "knock Saul of Tarsus off his horse." Acts never mentions a horse (Unit 4 rightly says "on foot or by donkey"). Suggested fix: "knock Saul of Tarsus to the ground."
12. **Unit 8 [b040], overstatement.** There is no record that "many of the people in that congregation lost their lives" in the Chicago Fire. Moody said he never saw that congregation together again. Suggested fix: "Moody never saw that congregation together again."
13. **Unit 10 [b031] and Unit 11 [b043], pastoral caution.** "Both of them died lost" (Felix and Agrippa) goes beyond what Scripture records. Suggested fix: "As far as Scripture tells us, neither ever believed."
14. **Unit 7 [b023]–[b025], illustration accuracy.** The Perlman "three strings" story is widely repeated but has never been confirmed (Perlman himself has not verified it). Consider adding "as the story is told."
15. **Unit 5 [b018], small.** *Tabitha qumi* and *Talitha cumi* differ by one letter, not "two syllables."
16. **Unit 1 [b029], small.** Acts 2:9–11 is usually counted as fifteen nations and regions, not sixteen.
