# Proposed Additions: Church Administration & Leadership (CTSAL)

Prepared for Dr. Wayne Cook's review. Nothing here has been changed in the course. Each addition is written so that it does not contradict the existing exams.

## Overall Assessment

This is a strong, warm, Scripture-centered course. Its main lines (steward, not owner; equip the saints; character before competence; restoration as the goal of discipline; multiplication and succession) are clear, practical, and pastoral, and the personal prison-ministry stories give it real authority. The biggest gaps are in four areas: (1) the course never sets out the three main models of church government; (2) it names the "lording it over" danger but never deals directly with authoritarian, "big man" leadership; (3) the full list of qualifications for overseers and deacons (1 Timothy 3; Titus 1) is only quoted in pieces; and (4) money and child-safety are taught as principles but are thin on the simple, concrete steps a small church with no accountant or lawyer can take tomorrow. Unit 1 also still uses KJV/RVG-style English and a different page layout from Units 2–10.

## Proposal 1 — Unit 2, after [b015]

**Title:** The "Big Man" Danger: When Shepherds Become Lords

**WHY:** In many churches across Latin America and Africa, the most common leadership failure is not laziness but domination, where one man controls everything and nobody can question him. Unit 2 warns against "lording it over" the flock, but it never names this pattern or gives the Bible's direct teaching on it.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> There is a kind of pastor every one of us has met. He is the "big man" of the church. Nothing happens without his word. No one may question him. The money, the keys, the pulpit, and every decision pass through his hands alone, and anyone who disagrees is quietly pushed aside or openly called rebellious. Sometimes he began humbly. But little by little the flock of God became his flock, and the servant became a lord.
>
> The Lord Jesus saw this coming and shut the door on it. "You know that those who are considered rulers over the Gentiles lord it over them, and their great ones exercise authority over them. Yet it shall not be so among you; but whoever desires to become great among you shall be your servant" (Mark 10:42-43). *It shall not be so among you.* The world's model of the powerful chief is not adjusted for the church; it is forbidden in it. And John gives us the portrait of the man who ignores that command: "Diotrephes, who loves to have the preeminence among them" (3 John 9). He rejected others, spoke against them with malicious words, and even put people out of the church. He wanted to be first, and the apostle names him so that every generation will recognize him.
>
> How do you know if you are drifting that way? Ask yourself honestly. Can anyone in my church tell me I am wrong without being punished for it? Do others share in the decisions and see the money? Am I training people who may one day lead better than I do, or do I feel threatened by them? Do I use "touch not the Lord's anointed" to silence honest questions? A true shepherd has nothing to fear from accountability. He welcomes it, because he knows the flock is not his and that he too must give account. Real spiritual authority is not seized by force. It is given by God and recognized by the people when a leader serves them the way Christ did.

## Proposal 2 — Unit 4, after [b009]

**Title:** Three Ways Churches Are Governed

**WHY:** Students will meet churches organized in very different ways, and a leader cannot build sound structure without knowing the main options. The CTS guardrails call for presenting episcopal, presbyterian, and congregational government fairly, and right now the course does not mention them at all.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> Before we go further, it helps to know that sincere, Bible-believing churches have organized themselves in three main ways. Each one points to real passages of Scripture.
>
> **Episcopal government** (from *episkopos*, "overseer" or "bishop") places churches under the oversight of bishops who care for many congregations. Its supporters point to the apostles' authority over many churches, and to Timothy and Titus, who were sent to set churches in order and to "appoint elders in every city" (Titus 1:5).
>
> **Presbyterian government** (from *presbyteros*, "elder") places each church under a group of elders, and joins churches together through larger councils of elders. Its supporters point to Paul and Barnabas appointing "elders in every church" (Acts 14:23), and to the Jerusalem council, where "the apostles and elders came together to consider this matter" (Acts 15:6).
>
> **Congregational government** gives the final human authority to the gathered members of the local church, who choose their leaders and make major decisions together. Its supporters point to the apostles telling the whole church, "seek out from among you seven men" (Acts 6:3), and to Jesus' word, "tell it to the church" (Matthew 18:17).
>
> Many churches combine these. A congregation may elect its elders, and those elders may meet with other churches for counsel. This course does not ask you to give up your own tradition. It asks you to understand the others with respect, and to notice what all three agree on: leadership is plural and accountable; no single person is above correction; leaders must meet the character standards of Scripture; and the whole body has a real part to play. "In the multitude of counselors there is safety" (Proverbs 11:14). Whatever form your church takes, make sure it has these marks, and make sure the form serves the mission, as we have just said, and never the other way around.

## Proposal 3 — Unit 5, after [b021]

**Title:** Simple Money Practices Any Church Can Follow

**WHY:** Unit 5 states the principle (more than one hand, open books) but gives a small church with no treasurer training nothing concrete to do. These practices cost nothing and protect both the church and the pastor.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> What does visible honesty look like in a small church with no accountant, no office, and perhaps no bank nearby? It does not take an expert. It takes a few simple habits, kept faithfully.
>
> **Count together.** At least two people who are not related to each other count every offering, right after the service, and both sign a written slip showing the date and the total.
>
> **Write it down the same day.** Keep one simple book, a notebook will do, with two columns: money in and money out. Every entry gets a date, an amount, and a short description.
>
> **Keep a receipt for every expense.** If a receipt is impossible, write a note of what was bought, for how much, and from whom, and have a second person sign it.
>
> **Never mix church money with personal money.** The church's funds are not kept in the pastor's pocket or the treasurer's house account. Where a bank account is possible, it should be in the church's name and need two signatures.
>
> **Report regularly.** Once a month, or at least once a quarter, give the members a short report: what came in, what went out, and what remains. Let any member see the book if they ask.
>
> **Have someone check.** Once a year, ask one or two trustworthy people who do not handle the money to go through the book and the receipts.
>
> The same rules apply to gifts from outside. If a church or a donor from another place sends help, account for it just as carefully, and report back to them how it was used. Be cautious of any person or group that offers money with strings attached, or asks you to send money in order to receive help.
>
> None of this means anyone is a thief. The Lord's own parable has the master say, "Give an account of your stewardship" (Luke 16:2). These habits simply let you give that account gladly, before God and before people, at any time.

## Proposal 4 — Unit 6, after [b017]

**Title:** The Biblical Qualifications for Overseers and Deacons

**WHY:** The course quotes pieces of 1 Timothy 3 in Units 5, 6, 8, and 10, but it never sets out the full lists in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1, which are the clearest standard the New Testament gives for choosing leaders. Students need the whole picture in one place.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> Paul does more than say "test them first." He gives the church a written standard, twice, so that no one has to guess. For overseers (also called bishops or elders): "A bishop then must be blameless, the husband of one wife, temperate, sober-minded, of good behavior, hospitable, able to teach; not given to wine, not violent, not greedy for money, but gentle, not quarrelsome, not covetous; one who rules his own house well... not a novice, lest being puffed up with pride he fall into the same condemnation as the devil" (1 Timothy 3:2-6). Titus 1:5-9 gives a very similar list and adds that the overseer must be "a steward of God, not self-willed, not quick-tempered... holding fast the faithful word." The deacons in 1 Timothy 3:8-13 must be "reverent, not double-tongued... not greedy for money, holding the mystery of the faith with a pure conscience."
>
> Read those lists slowly and notice how few of the items are about skill. Only one, "able to teach," is a gift. Nearly everything else is character: self-control, honesty, gentleness, freedom from the love of money, a well-ordered home, and a good name with outsiders. This is Jethro's pattern again: character first.
>
> Notice also two warnings that matter greatly for a growing church. "Not a novice": a new believer, however gifted, should not be rushed into leadership, because sudden position feeds pride. And "rules his own house well": a person's leadership at home is the training ground and the evidence for leadership in the church.
>
> Faithful Christians interpret some details differently, such as what exactly "husband of one wife" requires, and whether the "women" of 1 Timothy 3:11 are deacons' wives or women serving as deacons. This course does not settle those questions for you; follow your church's convictions with grace toward others. But on the heart of these lists, every church agrees. Use them. Read them aloud when leaders are chosen, and choose by them.

## Proposal 5 — Unit 7, after [b023]

**Title:** When the Accusation Is Against a Leader

**WHY:** Unit 7 teaches discipline for members, but small churches are most often torn apart when a pastor or elder is accused, and they are often unsure whether to ignore it, cover it, or act on rumor. 1 Timothy 5:19-21 gives clear guidance and also protects against discipline being used to silence critics.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> One situation needs special care: when the person accused of sin is a leader. Here churches fail in two opposite ways. Some believe every rumor and destroy a faithful servant's name in a week. Others protect the leader at all costs, silence those who raise concerns, and let real sin go on for years. Paul guards against both in a few verses.
>
> First, protect leaders from careless accusations: "Do not receive an accusation against an elder except from two or three witnesses" (1 Timothy 5:19). A leader is an easy target. Gossip, a grudge, or a misunderstanding must not be enough to remove him. Evidence must be real and confirmed.
>
> Second, do not protect sin because of a title: "Those who are sinning rebuke in the presence of all, that the rest also may fear" (1 Timothy 5:20). When a leader's sin is proven and continues, it is dealt with openly. Leadership raises the standard. It does not give a shield.
>
> Third, be fair to everyone: "I charge you before God and the Lord Jesus Christ and the elect angels that you observe these things without prejudice, doing nothing with partiality" (1 Timothy 5:21). It does not matter whether the accused is the founder, the pastor's relative, or the largest giver.
>
> In practice, this means a church should decide *before* any crisis who will hear a complaint against the pastor. It may be the other elders, the deacons, or trusted leaders from a related church or council. The pastor should never be the only judge of complaints about himself. And remember that church discipline is for sin, not for disagreement. A leader who uses "discipline" to remove people who question him has become Diotrephes, not a shepherd. When the matter does involve a crime, the church must also respect the civil authorities, as we will see in the next unit.

## Proposal 6 — Unit 8, after [b025]

**Title:** When a Concern Is Raised: Responding to Possible Abuse

**WHY:** Unit 8 rightly teaches prevention, but it does not tell leaders what to do when someone actually reports possible abuse of a child or vulnerable person. In that moment many churches make the worst mistake: they handle it quietly to protect the church's name. A short, clear set of steps could protect a child's life.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> Prevention is the first watch. But every leader must also know what to do if a child, or anyone vulnerable, tells of harm, or if someone reports a concern about a worker. That moment will test whether the church loves its reputation more than its lambs.
>
> **Believe enough to act.** Listen calmly. Thank the person for telling you. Do not promise to keep it secret, and do not question a child closely or try to investigate it yourself. Write down what was said, in the person's own words, with the date.
>
> **Protect first.** Make sure the person is safe right away. The accused worker should step back from all contact with children or vulnerable people while the matter is looked into. This is not a verdict; it is prudence.
>
> **Do not cover it.** Never handle a report of abuse privately "to avoid scandal," and never move the accused person quietly to another church or ministry. Abuse is not only a sin; it is a crime. Scripture calls the governing authority "God's minister to you for good" (Romans 13:4), and many nations require that such reports be made to the authorities. Learn what the law requires where you live, and obey it.
>
> **Care for the one who was harmed.** Stand with the victim and their family. Make sure they are heard, supported, and not blamed or pressured to "forgive and forget" quickly. Forgiveness is a real Christian duty, but it does not remove consequences or return a dangerous person to a place of trust.
>
> "Open your mouth for the speechless" (Proverbs 31:8). The little ones cannot defend themselves. The church that protects its abusers to save its name loses both its name and its witness. The church that protects its children, whatever it costs, shows the heart of the Good Shepherd.

## Errors or Problems Found

1. **Unit 1, [b007], [b008], [b011], [b012], [b017], [b018], [b025]: wrong English Bible version.** Unit 1 labels its quotations "RVG" and quotes in KJV-style English ("What is this thing that thou doest," "hearkened," "It is not reason that we should leave the word of God"). The CTS standard for English is NKJV. [b015] already uses NKJV wording, so the unit is inconsistent within itself. Unit 1 also uses an older page layout ("Catalog," "Check Multiple Choice") from Units 2–10, with no Professor/Director line.
2. **Unit 1, [b028]: small factual slip.** The text says that when Moses obeyed, "the text gives us a small line... the people 'went to their place in peace.'" In Exodus 18:23 this is Jethro's *promise* before Moses acts ("all this people will also go to their place in peace," NKJV), not a report after Moses obeyed. A suggested fix: "Jethro's counsel ends with a small promise that is easy to read past: the people 'will also go to their place in peace.'"
3. **Unit 3, [b005] and [b010]: Proverbs 29:18 quoted from the KJV but labeled NKJV.** "Where there is no vision, the people perish" is KJV wording. The NKJV reads: "Where there is no revelation, the people cast off restraint; but happy is he who keeps the law." [b010] already explains that "vision" means "revelation" and mentions casting off restraint, so switching the epigraph to the NKJV fits the teaching better. Check the exams first, since a question may quote "perish."
4. **Unit 5, [b007]: a claim that is often repeated but disputed.** "Our Lord spoke about money constantly — more than He spoke of heaven, more than He spoke of prayer." This is a popular claim, but word counts do not clearly support it. Safer wording: "Our Lord spoke about money and possessions again and again, in parable after parable."
5. **Unit 5, [b021]: "Avoid even the appearance of evil"** echoes the KJV of 1 Thessalonians 5:22. The NKJV reads "Abstain from every form of evil." This is fine as a paraphrase as long as it is not presented as a quotation.
6. **Unit 4, [b011]: unclear phrase.** "the punctuation of how we live it makes all the difference" refers to the well-known comma debate in Ephesians 4:12 without explaining it, and average readers will not follow it. Suggested: "Read that slowly, because how we read it changes how we live it."
7. **Throughout (for example Unit 2 [b022], Unit 10 [b007], [b030]): male-only address.** Leaders are always "brother" and "he." This is not an error, but where a unit speaks to all church leaders (volunteers, teachers, treasurers), an occasional "brother or sister" would fit the CTS neutral stance on women in ministry better.
