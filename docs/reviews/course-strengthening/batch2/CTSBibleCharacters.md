# Proposed Additions — Bible Characters I (CTSBibleCharacters)

For Dr. Wayne Cook's approval. Nothing below changes existing text or tests.

## Assessment

This is a warm, sermonic, Christ-centered course. Each unit has a clear subject and proposition, the Scripture is handled with reverence, and the illustrations are mostly personal and well within the 20% limit. Its main gap is the Genesis family material: polygamy, the surrogate-wife custom, the birthright and blessing, and favoritism passed from parent to child. These are exactly the issues pastors in Africa and Latin America deal with. The units mention them only in passing and never explain the ancient background or apply it to the reader's own setting. Two well-known episodes are missing completely: Gideon's fleece, which is widely misused as a way to find God's will, and Gideon's sad ending. Job would also benefit from a pastoral word on the "theology of blame" that is common today in curse, witchcraft and prosperity teaching. Most units run 1,650–1,750 words, well under the 2,500-word target, so each addition below fits without making a unit too long.

## Proposals

### 1. Unit 2 (Job), after [b016] — When Job's Friends Come to Church

**WHY:** Job's friends taught that suffering always means hidden sin. Today the same idea comes as "someone has cursed you," "there is sin in your bloodline," or "you lack faith for prosperity," and pastors in our students' regions meet it every week. This paragraph applies the unit's second point directly and shows the friends' one good act, their silence.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

Before we move on, notice that Job's friends began well. When they first came, "they sat down with him on the ground seven days and seven nights, and no one spoke a word to him, for they saw that his grief was very great" (Job 2:13, NKJV). That silence was the best ministry they gave him. The trouble began when they opened their mouths to explain his suffering. Their theology was simple and tidy: good people prosper, bad people suffer, so Job must have done something wrong. And at the end of the book God said to them, "You have not spoken of Me what is right, as My servant Job has" (Job 42:7, NKJV).

Job's friends are still with us, and some of them stand in pulpits. In many places, when a child dies or a business fails or sickness will not leave, someone says at once, "Who has cursed you? What sin is hidden in your family? Where is your faith?" Some preachers promise that a believer with enough faith will never be sick or poor, so the sufferer is left with two wounds: the loss itself and the shame of being blamed for it. Pastor, do not be that kind of comforter. Scripture does teach that sin can bring trouble, but it never teaches that every trouble proves sin. A believer in Christ need not live in fear of curses, for "a curse without cause shall not alight" (Proverbs 26:2, NKJV), and "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13, NKJV). When you visit the suffering, sit with them first. Weep with those who weep. Pray before you explain, and be slow to explain at all. Sometimes the most faithful thing a shepherd can say is, "I do not know why this has happened, but I know the God who is with you in it."

*(about 300 words)*

### 2. Unit 3 (Abraham), after [b021] — Hagar: An Ancient Custom and Its Cost

**WHY:** At present [b021] mentions Hagar in one sentence. Explaining the ancient surrogate-wife custom shows readers why Sarah's plan seemed reasonable, and the story then shows the cost of having more than one wife. It also shows God's care for the outcast, which matters wherever plural marriage and "outside children" are common.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

To understand Sarah's plan, we need to know the customs of her world. Ancient documents from the region show that a childless wife could give her servant to her husband, and the child of that union would be counted as hers. So when Sarah said, "Please, go in to my maid; perhaps I shall obtain children by her" (Genesis 16:2, NKJV), she was not suggesting something shocking to her neighbors. She was doing what sensible people of her day did. That is exactly the danger. A custom can be normal, legal and widely practiced and still not be God's way of keeping God's promise.

Look at what followed. As soon as Hagar conceived, she despised her mistress. Sarah blamed Abraham, then treated Hagar so harshly that she fled into the desert. Years later the rivalry between the two mothers and their sons ended with Hagar and Ishmael sent away. Genesis does not lecture us here. It simply tells the truth and lets us watch the jealousy, the blame and the broken hearts. Many pastors serve where a man may have more than one wife, or where children of other unions grow up beside the children of the home. This story speaks to those households honestly. Wherever God's design of one man and one woman is set aside, rivalry and grief are rarely far behind.

Yet the most beautiful part of the chapter belongs to Hagar. Alone, pregnant and rejected, she met the Angel of the LORD by a spring in the wilderness. He called her by name, gave her a promise for her son, and sent her back with hope. And this foreign servant woman gave God a name no one had given Him before: "You-Are-the-God-Who-Sees" (Genesis 16:13, NKJV). The God of Abraham is also the God of the woman pushed aside. No one in your congregation is too small or too far outside the family for Him to see.

*(about 340 words)*

### 3. Unit 4 (Rebekah), after [b030] — Birthright and Blessing: What Was at Stake

**WHY:** The unit tells the birthright and blessing story but never explains what the birthright was or why Isaac's blessing could not be taken back. Many students come from cultures with strong beliefs about a parent's or an ancestor's blessing and curse, and they need to see what the passage teaches and what it does not.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

To feel the weight of Esau's choice, we need to understand what he gave away. In the ancient world the birthright belonged to the firstborn son. It brought a double share of the father's property (Deuteronomy 21:17) and the right to lead the family after the father died. In Abraham's family it meant even more: the line of the covenant promise. The father's blessing, spoken near the end of his life, was a solemn word that everyone treated as binding. That is why Isaac, trembling when he discovered the deception, said of Jacob, "I have blessed him — and indeed he shall be blessed" (Genesis 27:33, NKJV). Esau traded away the first and lost the second, and Hebrews says "he found no place for repentance, though he sought it diligently with tears" (Hebrews 12:17, NKJV).

Many of our students live where a father's blessing, or a dying grandmother's curse, is believed to carry great power for generations. What does this story teach? It teaches that parents' words matter a great deal. Fathers and mothers should bless their children often and deliberately, speaking God's promises over them. But it does not teach that human words are magic. Isaac's blessing stood because it agreed with what God had already said: "the older shall serve the younger." The power was in God's word, not in Isaac's breath. No ancestor, witch or angry relative can curse what God has blessed: "For there is no sorcery against Jacob, nor any divination against Israel" (Numbers 23:23, NKJV). God also told His people plainly, "The son shall not bear the guilt of the father" (Ezekiel 18:20, NKJV). The believer in Christ stands under the blessing of the Father, and no word spoken against him can overturn it.

### 4. Unit 5 (Joseph), after [b012] — Favoritism Handed Down

**WHY:** Right now [b012] says Jacob's favoritism "bred trouble," but the course never connects it to the pattern running through four generations. That pattern includes Isaac and Rebekah's divided love and the rival wives in Jacob's own house. It is very practical teaching for families, and especially for homes with more than one wife.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

Jacob's favoritism did not come from nowhere. He had grown up in a divided home, where "Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game, but Rebekah loved Jacob" (Genesis 25:28, NKJV). He knew the pain of being one parent's favorite and the other's disappointment, and the hatred it stirred between brothers. Yet when he had his own family, he repeated the pattern, and made it worse. Jacob married two sisters, Leah and Rachel, and took their two servants as wives as well. He "loved Rachel more than Leah" (Genesis 29:30, NKJV), and the home became a battlefield of rival mothers, each naming her children after her own sorrow or victory. When Rachel's son Joseph was born, Jacob loved him most of all, "because he was the son of his old age" (Genesis 37:3, NKJV). The ornate robe was only the outward sign of a preference everyone in the tent already felt.

Genesis tells these things without approving them. It shows us what favoritism and divided households produce: envy, hatred, deception and, at last, brothers willing to sell their own flesh and blood. Here is a word for every parent and every pastor. Children always know who is favored. The wounds of a divided home often pass to the next generation unless someone, by God's grace, breaks the pattern. In some places a man may have several wives, and the children of each mother compete for the father's love and inheritance. In other places the favored child may be the son over the daughter, the light-skinned over the dark, or the educated over the one who stayed home. The cure is the same everywhere: love each child openly and fairly, and repent of favoritism when you see it. And when you see the wounds it has left in others, point them to the God who, even in Jacob's broken family, "saw that Leah was unloved" (Genesis 29:31, NKJV) and drew near to her.

*(about 330 words)*

### 5. Unit 7 (Gideon), after [b031] — The Fleece, and How to Read It

**WHY:** Gideon's fleece (Judges 6:36–40) is the best-known part of his story and is missing from the unit. Many believers "put out a fleece" to make decisions. This episode is the ideal place to teach the difference between what the Bible describes and what it commands. The added text fits the unit's own theme that "God was gentle with his fear."

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

This was not the first time God had stooped to Gideon's weakness. Before the army ever gathered, Gideon had asked for a sign: "Look, I shall put a fleece of wool on the threshing floor; if there is dew on the fleece only, and it is dry on all the ground, then I shall know that You will save Israel by my hand, as You have said" (Judges 6:37, NKJV). God did it. Then Gideon, still afraid, said, "Do not be angry with me, but let me speak just once more," and asked for the opposite sign, a dry fleece on wet ground. And God did that too.

Many believers today speak of "putting out a fleece" to decide whom to marry, which job to take, or whether to move. We must read this passage carefully. Notice the words "as You have said." God had already told Gideon plainly what to do. The fleece was not a way to discover God's will; it was a request for courage to obey a will already known. And notice that Gideon himself feared the request might anger God. The Bible records the fleece honestly, but it never commands us to copy it. Here is an important rule for preaching any Bible character: not everything a character did is set before us as an example. Some things are described so that we may learn from them; only what God commands is prescribed for us to do.

What the fleece teaches is not a method but a merciful God. He was patient with a fearful man who wanted to obey but trembled. We have something Gideon did not: the whole written Word of God and the Holy Spirit within us. We need not test God with signs. We can open His Word, pray, seek godly counsel, and step out in faith. And when we tremble, we can trust that the same patient Lord will strengthen us to obey.

*(about 330 words)*

### 6. Unit 7 (Gideon), after [b035] — The Rest of Gideon's Story: Finishing Well

**WHY:** The unit stops at the victory. Judges 8–9, however, tells how Gideon later made an ephod that "became a snare," and how his many wives led to Abimelech's slaughter of seventy brothers. That ending is an important warning for leaders about pride after success and about plural marriage. It does not contradict the unit's theme, because it shows what happens when a man forgets that the battle was the Lord's.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

We would like the story to end at the shout and the shattered pitchers, but the Bible tells us the rest, and leaders need to hear it. After the victory, the men of Israel asked Gideon to rule over them, and he gave a noble answer: "I will not rule over you, nor shall my son rule over you; the LORD shall rule over you" (Judges 8:23, NKJV). Yet in the very next breath he asked for the gold earrings from the spoil, and from them he made an ephod, a priestly garment, and set it up in his town. "And all Israel played the harlot with it there. It became a snare to Gideon and to his house" (Judges 8:27, NKJV). The man who had torn down his father's idol at the beginning of his story left behind a new object of false worship at the end.

There was more. "Gideon had seventy sons who were his own offspring, for he had many wives. And his concubine who was in Shechem also bore him a son, whose name he called Abimelech" (Judges 8:30–31, NKJV). That name means "my father is king," which is strange for the son of a man who refused to be king. After Gideon died, Abimelech murdered his seventy brothers on a single stone to seize power. Only the youngest, Jotham, escaped. The divided household of the hero became a field of blood.

Here is a sober lesson for every pastor and leader. The most dangerous hour is often not the battle but the season after the victory, when people praise us and the gifts pour in. Gideon began in a winepress, humble and afraid; he ended with gold, many wives and a snare. "Therefore let him who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12, NKJV). It is not enough to begin well. By God's grace, let us finish well too.

*(about 330 words)*

## Errors or problems found

1. **Unit 1 [b035]:** "Five times in Genesis 5 a refrain tolls ... 'and he died.'" In fact "and he died" appears **eight** times in Genesis 5 (Adam, Seth, Enosh, Cainan, Mahalalel, Jared, Methuselah, Lamech). Suggested fix: "Eight times," or "Again and again." Check first whether an exam item uses "five"; if one does, the text and the item need to change together.
2. **Unit 1 [b007]:** The paragraph lists "Adam in the garden" among those Scripture says "walked with God." Genesis says only that God was "walking in the garden" (Genesis 3:8). The phrase "walked with God" is used of Enoch (Genesis 5:22, 24) and Noah (Genesis 6:9). Suggest removing Adam or rewording, e.g. "Adam once enjoyed God's company in the garden."
3. **Unit 12 [b016] and [b021] are placed in the wrong sections.** [b016] talks about the brook and the widow ("this second movement") but sits under heading I, before "II. The God Who Provides" [b017]. [b021] talks about the water drenching at Carmel but sits under heading II, before "III. Fire on Mount Carmel" [b022], so readers meet it before the story is told. Suggest moving [b016] to follow [b020] and [b021] to follow [b025]. [b026] (the collapse) also comes just before heading IV; it reads acceptably as a transition but would fit better after [b028].
4. **Unit 4 [b011]/[b012]:** The block ids are out of order. The heading [b012] "I. The Bride Sought at the Well" comes before paragraph [b011]. This is cosmetic only, so check that nothing depends on the ids being in sequence.
5. **Unit 2 [b015]:** "God did not strike Job's family, did not cause his sores" is accurate about who acted (Job 2:7). But the book also says God allowed it and was "incited ... against him" (Job 2:3), and Job 42:11 speaks of "the adversity that the LORD had brought upon him." Consider adding one clause, e.g. "...though nothing came without His permission," so careful readers do not see a contradiction. This is optional.
6. **Unit 2 [b017]:** Job 22:21 is quoted as something one of the friends "said rightly." The speaker is Eliphaz, whom God rebukes in 42:7. The verse is true in itself, but "rightly" may confuse students; consider "As even Eliphaz rightly said..."
7. **Unit 2 [b020]:** The text says Rockefeller "lived to ninety-eight." He lived 1839–1937 and died at 97, about two months before turning 98. Suggest "lived to nearly ninety-eight."
8. **Minor wording points (optional):** Unit 4 [b033] says Rebekah "would die without ever seeing her son again." This is a sound inference, but Scripture does not state it; consider adding "as far as Scripture records." Unit 8 [b024] says Obed means "worship"; it is more precisely "servant" or "one who serves (worships)." Unit 8 [b007] (Franklin) and Unit 2 [b022] (Finney's "Then what?") are popular stories whose details are uncertain. Unit 6 [b029] already handles a similar story with "as the story goes," and the same hedge could be used here. Unit 2 [b017] has a stray space in "patience :", and Unit 3 [b014]/[b019], Unit 7 [b018] and Unit 11 [b018]/[b020]/[b021] have similar spaces before punctuation, probably left over from removed bold or italics.

None of the content guardrails are breached: Sodom is handled without forbidden topics, CTS is never called Baptist, and no narrative is called "myth."
