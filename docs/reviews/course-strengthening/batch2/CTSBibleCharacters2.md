# Bible Characters II — Proposed Additions for Dr. Cook's Review

## Assessment

Bible Characters II is warm, Christ-centered, and plainly sermonic. Each unit has a clear subject and proposition, solid NKJV quotation, and an honest, pastoral handling of doubt, failure, and restoration. Its main gaps are in three areas. First, it offers little guidance on preaching a character fairly, especially where a passage could be misused (Hosea's marriage, Ezra's mixed marriages). Second, a few historical anchors that defend the narrative as real history are missing (Jonah in 2 Kings 14:25). Third, it rarely speaks directly to pressures pastors face in Latin America and Africa, such as prosperity preaching, receiving converts with violent pasts, and inherited traditions about Mary Magdalene. Most units run about 1,650 words, well under the ~2,500-word target, so each proposal below fits without crowding the unit. None of them changes a fact the exams rely on.

## Proposals

### 1. Unit 1 (Jonah), after [b012]
**Title:** Jonah Was a Real Prophet in Real History

**WHY:** The unit mentions people who laugh at Jonah as "a mere fish tale" (b007) but never gives students the biblical evidence that Jonah was a historical prophet. Pastors need that evidence when skeptics, or seminary-trained critics, call the book a parable.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**
> Before we go further, we should settle one question that many people raise: was Jonah a real man, or only a story told to teach a lesson? The Bible answers plainly. Jonah appears not only in the book that bears his name but also in the history of the kings of Israel, where he is named as a working prophet in the days of King Jeroboam II, about eight centuries before Christ.
>
> He restored the territory of Israel from the entrance of Hamath to the Sea of the Arabah, according to the word of the LORD God of Israel, which He had spoken through His servant Jonah the son of Amittai, the prophet who was from Gath Hepher. (2 Kings 14:25, NKJV)
>
> Jonah had a father, a hometown in Galilee, and a ministry recorded alongside a known king. More important still, the Lord Jesus spoke of Jonah and of Nineveh as real history. He said, "The men of Nineveh will rise up in the judgment with this generation and condemn it, because they repented at the preaching of Jonah; and indeed a greater than Jonah is here" (Matthew 12:41). Imaginary people do not rise up at the judgment. Jesus put Jonah's three days in the fish beside His own three days in the grave. If one was only a tale, what would that say about the other? We do not need to be embarrassed by this book. The God who made the sea and all that is in it is able to prepare a great fish, and He is able to raise His Son from the dead. When people in your community laugh at Jonah, do not argue about fish. Point them to the One who believed this story and who rose from the dead Himself.

### 2. Unit 2 (Hosea), after [b030]
**Title:** Preaching Hosea Fairly — A Prophetic Sign, Not a Rule for Every Marriage

**WHY:** Hosea's command to take back an unfaithful wife can be misused to pressure a spouse, often a wife, to stay in danger or to excuse a partner who keeps sinning without repentance. Pastors need help separating what this text describes from what it prescribes.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**
> A word of caution for those of us who preach. Hosea's marriage was a special command given to one prophet as a living sign to a nation: "Go again, love a woman... just like the love of the LORD for the children of Israel" (Hosea 3:1). The Bible describes what God told Hosea to do. It does not make his marriage a rule that every betrayed husband or wife must follow in the same way. We must be careful here, because in many of our churches a suffering wife comes to the pastor, and he quotes Hosea to send her back into a home where she is beaten, threatened, or shamed again and again. That is not the message of this book.
>
> The main point of Hosea is about God's heart, not about marriage policy. It shows us how God loves His unfaithful people. Its application for every believer is to forgive from the heart and to pursue reconciliation where repentance makes it possible. But forgiveness and safety are not enemies. A pastor can urge forgiveness and still protect the vulnerable, call the sinning spouse to genuine repentance, and bring in wise church leaders to walk with the family. Notice that even in Hosea's story there was a period of waiting and of changed behavior: "You shall stay with me many days; you shall not play the harlot" (Hosea 3:3). Restoration was real, but it was not careless.
>
> So preach Hosea with tears and with joy, for it is the gospel of relentless grace. Let it humble every one of us as Gomer, the unfaithful one who has been bought back. But never turn this beautiful picture of God's love into a weapon against the wounded.

### 3. Unit 3 (Jeremiah), after [b026]
**Title:** Jeremiah and the Prophets of "Peace, Peace"

**WHY:** The unit does not mention Jeremiah's main opponents, the popular false prophets such as Hananiah who promised prosperity and quick deliverance. This is one of the most relevant lessons for pastors facing prosperity preaching in Africa and Latin America, and it also puts the often-quoted Jeremiah 29:11 in its context.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**
> Jeremiah's hardest opponents were not pagans but preachers. Jerusalem was full of popular prophets who told the people exactly what they wanted to hear: no judgment is coming, God is pleased with you, blessing is just around the corner. God's verdict on them was severe.
>
> For they have healed the hurt of the daughter of My people slightly, saying, "Peace, peace!" when there is no peace. (Jeremiah 8:11, NKJV)
>
> One of them, Hananiah, stood up in the temple and broke the wooden yoke off Jeremiah's neck. He announced that within two years Babylon would be broken and everything would be restored. The crowd surely preferred his sermon. But Jeremiah answered, "Hear now, Hananiah, the LORD has not sent you, but you make this people trust in a lie" (Jeremiah 28:15). Within the year Hananiah was dead.
>
> Every generation has its Hananiahs, and many of our nations know them well. Some preachers promise wealth, healing, and victory to anyone who gives enough, and they never speak of repentance, suffering, or the cross. Jeremiah teaches us that a message is not true because it is pleasant, popular, or followed by large crowds. It is true when it comes from the mouth of the LORD.
>
> Even the beloved promise "For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, says the LORD, thoughts of peace and not of evil, to give you a future and a hope" (Jeremiah 29:11) was written to exiles who were told to settle in Babylon for seventy years (Jeremiah 29:7, 10). God's hope was real, but it came through hardship, not around it. A faithful pastor offers people that same honest hope.

### 4. Unit 5 (Ezra), after [b031]
**Title:** Preaching Ezra 10 Carefully — Faithfulness, Not Divorce or Tribe

**WHY:** Ezra's call to put away foreign wives can be misread today as permission to divorce an unbelieving spouse, or as a mandate for ethnic or tribal separation. Pastors need the New Testament guidance that keeps the application faithful and safe.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**
> Pastors must handle this chapter with special care, because it is easy to misapply. The problem in Ezra's day was not race or nationality. It was idolatry. The leaders confessed that the people had joined themselves to the nations "with respect to the abominations" of the Canaanites and others (Ezra 9:1). The issue was a covenant people mixing the worship of the living God with the worship of idols, the very sin that had sent them into exile. Remember that Rahab the Canaanite and Ruth the Moabitess were welcomed into Israel and even into the family line of the Messiah, because they turned to the LORD. God's concern has always been faith, never skin, tribe, or language. Any preacher who uses Ezra to justify tribal division or ethnic pride has missed the point entirely.
>
> Nor does Ezra 10 tell a believer today to divorce an unbelieving husband or wife. The New Testament speaks directly to that situation:
>
> If any brother has a wife who does not believe, and she is willing to live with him, let him not divorce her. And a woman who has a husband who does not believe, if he is willing to live with her, let her not divorce him. (1 Corinthians 7:12-13, NKJV)
>
> What does carry over from Ezra is the call to wholehearted faithfulness. Single believers should marry "only in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 7:39) and not be "unequally yoked together with unbelievers" (2 Corinthians 6:14). All of us are called to make a clean break with the idols of our own culture, whether they are ancestral spirits, the saints and charms of folk religion, or the love of money. Ezra calls us to radical repentance. The gospel shows us what to cut off: the sin, never the people God has given us to love.

### 5. Unit 10 (Mary Magdalene), after [b010]
**Title:** Setting the Record Straight About Mary Magdalene

**WHY:** Many students, especially those from Roman Catholic or popular-religion backgrounds in Latin America, have been taught that Mary Magdalene was a repentant prostitute. Scripture never says this, and pastors should be able to explain where the idea came from and why it matters.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**
> We should also clear away a long-standing mistake. For many centuries, in sermons, paintings, and films, Mary Magdalene has been pictured as a former prostitute. Many of us grew up hearing that. But the Bible never says it. The idea came largely from a sermon preached by Pope Gregory the Great in the year 591. He joined together three different women in the Gospels: the unnamed "woman in the city who was a sinner" who anointed Jesus' feet (Luke 7:37), Mary of Bethany, and Mary Magdalene. Luke, however, introduces Mary Magdalene in the very next chapter as a new person, with a different story.
>
> ...and certain women who had been healed of evil spirits and infirmities — Mary called Magdalene, out of whom had come seven demons. (Luke 8:2, NKJV)
>
> Her name tells us where she was from, Magdala, a fishing town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. Her testimony was deliverance from demonic bondage, not a life of immorality. This matters for at least two reasons. First, faithful preachers should say only what the text says, and not repeat tradition as if it were Scripture. Second, it speaks powerfully to our people. In many of our communities, men and women live in fear of spirits, curses, and witchcraft. Mary Magdalene is the living proof that Christ has full authority over every power of darkness. The One who freed her from seven demons can set anyone free. Freed women and men, like her, become His most devoted followers.

### 6. Unit 11 (Paul), after [b018]
**Title:** Barnabas — The Man Who Opened the Door for Saul

**WHY:** The unit tells of Ananias but not of Barnabas, without whom the church might never have received its former persecutor. For pastors receiving converts with violent or criminal pasts, a common reality in both regions, this is essential, practical application.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**
> Ananias was not the only brave believer God used. When the newly converted Saul came to Jerusalem, the church did not welcome him with open arms.
>
> And when Saul had come to Jerusalem, he tried to join the disciples; but they were all afraid of him, and did not believe that he was a disciple. But Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles. And he declared to them how he had seen the Lord on the road. (Acts 9:26-27, NKJV)
>
> Who can blame them? The man had dragged their friends to prison and stood approving at Stephen's death. But one man, Barnabas, whose name means "Son of Encouragement" (Acts 4:36), took the risk. He listened to Saul's story, believed God's grace was real in him, and stood beside him before the apostles. Years later, it was Barnabas again who went looking for Saul in Tarsus and brought him to Antioch to teach (Acts 11:25-26). Without Barnabas, the church might have lost its greatest missionary.
>
> Every pastor will one day face a Saul of his own: a former gang member, a man who served in an armed group, a woman known in the whole town for her sins, an old persecutor of the church who now says he has met Christ. Our first instinct may be fear, and some caution is wise. Barnabas did not simply take Saul's word; he heard his testimony and saw the fruit of his preaching in Damascus. But caution must not become a closed door. If grace can make a persecutor into an apostle, the church must be ready to receive what grace has made. May God give every congregation a Barnabas, and may He make each of us one.

## Errors or problems found

1. **Unit 9 [b012]:** "The message came back at last in three plain and crushing words: 'Lazarus is dead' (John 11:14)." This misattributes the verse. In John 11:14 no message comes back to Jesus; Jesus Himself tells the disciples plainly, "Lazarus is dead." Suggested wording: "Then Jesus told His disciples plainly, in three crushing words: 'Lazarus is dead' (John 11:14)."
2. **Unit 6 [b024]:** "sat down under a juniper tree." The NKJV reads "broom tree" (1 Kings 19:4). "Juniper" is the KJV wording.
3. **Unit 5 [b023]:** "he appears more than sixty years later." Ezra arrived about 458 BC, roughly 57 years after the temple was finished (515 BC) and about 80 years after the first return (538 BC). Suggested wording: "nearly sixty years after the temple was finished."
4. **Unit 4 [b013]:** Two statistics cannot be verified and may draw challenges: "roughly one hundred and twenty-five commands... twenty-one of them" and "a study from Johns Hopkins." Also, Black Bart is usually credited with about 28 stagecoach robberies, not 29. Consider wording such as "many of the commands" and "studies have suggested."
5. **Unit 11 [b023]:** "From Antioch he sailed across Asia Minor." Paul crossed Asia Minor mostly on foot. Suggested wording: "traveled across Asia Minor."
6. **Unit 10 [b024]:** "The first preacher of the resurrection was this woman." Given the course's neutral stance on women in ministry, "the first witness" or "the first herald" (wording the paragraph already uses) avoids seeming to take a side on the debate. This is optional.
7. **Unit 2 [b021], [b023]:** "sold into slavery" and "the slave market" are a common and reasonable interpretation of Hosea 3:2, but the text does not state them outright. Consider adding "it appears" so students know this is interpretation.
