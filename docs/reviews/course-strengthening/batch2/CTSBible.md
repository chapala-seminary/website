# How We Got the Bible (CTSBible) — Proposed Additions

## Assessment

This is a strong, well-built course. Its "four witnesses" structure, the camel-and-gnat discipline, and its fair handling of the Apocrypha and the Received/Critical Text question are exactly right for pastors and lay leaders. The largest gap is the step from manuscript to the reader's own language. The course says many times that "the authority travels into the copy and the translation," but it never tells the story of the translators (Jerome, Tyndale, Reina and Valera). It also never helps a pastor in Latin America or Africa choose among the Spanish, French, Portuguese or Swahili Bibles his people actually carry. RVR 1960 is the most widely used Spanish Bible, and the course never mentions it. Two practical gaps follow from this. The course gives no answer to "the Bible was changed," the most common objection in many African and some Latin American settings. It also gives no pastoral help for the church member who is upset by a footnote in his Bible. A few factual and NKJV-wording problems are listed at the end. None of them is serious.

## Proposals

### 1. Unit 6, after [b011] — From the Original Languages into Ours: The Translators

**WHY:** The course is called "How We Got the Bible," yet it moves from manuscripts straight to printed Greek texts. It never tells how the Word reached the common believer's language, and it never tells the story of the Reina-Valera that most students hold.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> **From the Original Languages into Ours: The Translators**
>
> A Greek text on a scholar's desk does no good to a farmer who reads only Spanish or Swahili. So the story of how we got the Bible is also the story of the translators. And translation is nothing new. Even before Christ, Jews who no longer spoke Hebrew read the Scriptures in Greek. Around AD 400, Jerome put the Bible into the everyday Latin of his time. That is the Vulgate, which means "common." Over the centuries, Latin itself stopped being the language of ordinary people. Then God raised up men who paid dearly to give His Word back to them. John Wycliffe's followers put the Bible into English in the late 1300s. William Tyndale translated the New Testament from the Greek and printed it in 1526, and he was put to death for it in 1536. His work flowed into the King James Version of 1611. In Spain, Casiodoro de Reina, a former monk who had fled the Inquisition, published the first complete Spanish Bible in 1569. It is called the *Biblia del Oso*, the "Bear Bible," for the picture on its title page. In 1602 Cipriano de Valera revised it, and that Reina-Valera, revised again in 1862, 1909 and 1960 and in later editions, has fed the Spanish-speaking church for over four hundred years. The French-speaking churches of Africa read the work of the Swiss pastor Louis Segond. The Swahili churches of East Africa read the Union Version, completed in the mid-twentieth century. Notice the pattern. At Pentecost the crowd marveled, "how is it that we hear, each in our own language in which we were born?" (Acts 2:8). God has never been content to speak only in one sacred tongue. The translators were not adding to the Word. They were carrying it home.

*(about 300 words)*

### 2. Unit 6, after [b023] — Choosing a Bible for Your People

**WHY:** Pastors constantly face the practical question "Which Bible should our church use?" The course argues the textual question well but gives no help with translation *method* (word-for-word vs. thought-for-thought) or with the actual versions in the students' languages.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> **Choosing a Bible for Your People**
>
> The question of which Greek text to follow is only half the matter. The other half is how a translation renders it. Some versions aim to be word-for-word, keeping as close as possible to the form of the original: the King James, the New King James, the Reina-Valera, the *Nueva Biblia de las Américas* (NBLA). Others aim to be thought-for-thought, giving the meaning in natural modern speech: the NIV and the Spanish NVI, and simpler still, *Dios Habla Hoy* or the *Nueva Traducción Viviente*. Both aims are honest. A word-for-word Bible serves careful study and preaching well. A thought-for-thought Bible may serve a new believer, a child, or a reader with little schooling. Many pastors keep one of each on the desk.
>
> In the Spanish-speaking world, the Reina-Valera 1960 is the Bible most churches know and love. This course uses the Reina-Valera Gómez for its close agreement with the Received Text, and many readers also value the NBLA and the NVI. French-speaking churches in Africa have long used the Louis Segond, now also available in the updated *Segond 21*. Swahili-speaking churches read the Union Version (*Biblia Takatifu*), and many also use the plain-language *Biblia Habari Njema*. Portuguese speakers in Brazil, Angola and Mozambique have the Almeida.
>
> How should a pastor choose? Ask four plain questions. Is it faithful, made by translators who honor the Bible as God's Word? Is it clear, so that my people can understand it when it is read aloud? Is it trusted, a version my congregation and its neighbors recognize? Is it available, something my people can actually buy and carry? Then, whatever you choose, do what Ezra's helpers did: "they read distinctly from the book, in the Law of God; and they gave the sense, and helped them to understand the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8). And never let the choice of a version divide brothers who love the same Lord.

*(about 340 words)*

### 3. Unit 5, after [b023] — "But the Bible Was Changed": A Word for Conversations with Muslim Neighbors

**WHY:** Across much of Africa, and more and more in Latin America, the most common objection a pastor will hear is the Muslim teaching that the Bible was corrupted. The manuscripts in this unit answer it directly and gently, but the course never makes the connection.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> **"But the Bible Was Changed": A Word for Conversations with Muslim Neighbors**
>
> Many of our students live beside Muslim neighbors, and sooner or later one will say kindly, "We honor the Torah and the Gospel, but your Bible has been changed." How should a pastor answer? First, with respect, for Peter tells us to "give a defense to everyone who asks you a reason for the hope that is in you, with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15). Then, with the witnesses of this very unit. Muhammad lived from about AD 570 to 632. Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus were copied around three centuries before he was born. The papyri are older still, and the Dead Sea Scrolls are older than Christ Himself. So we can ask a fair and simple question: if the Bible was changed, when? If it was changed before Muhammad's day, then the manuscripts from those centuries should show it. Yet they carry the same Gospels and the same Law we read today. If it was changed after his day, then the older manuscripts give us the unchanged text, and it says what our Bibles say. Notice also that the Qur'an itself speaks of the Torah and the Gospel as Scriptures that people in its own day could read and consult. Muslim teachers explain those verses in different ways, and we should not pretend the conversation ends there. But the manuscript evidence is open for anyone to examine. Here, as always, we refuse to overclaim. These manuscripts do not prove that the Bible is God's Word. They do show that it was not rewritten in the centuries the charge supposes. Answer gently, invite your neighbor to read the Gospel for himself, and trust the Word to speak for itself: "the word of our God stands forever" (Isaiah 40:8).

*(about 320 words)*

### 4. Unit 4, after [b025] — When a Church Member Finds a Footnote

**WHY:** The unit explains variants well in principle. But the moment a pastor actually faces is a member pointing to a bracket or a note in his Bible ("Some manuscripts do not contain...") and asking whether the Bible is wrong. A short pastoral word turns the theory into practice.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> **When a Church Member Finds a Footnote**
>
> Sooner or later a member of your church will come to you troubled. "Pastor, my Bible has a note here that says 'some manuscripts omit this verse.' Does that mean the Bible is not sure of itself?" How you answer that moment matters more than any lecture. Begin by thanking him for reading carefully. The Bereans were commended because they "searched the Scriptures daily to find out whether these things were so" (Acts 17:11). Then explain plainly what the note is. It is not a confession of error. It is an act of honesty. The translators are telling the reader openly where the old copies differ, so that nothing is hidden. A Bible with footnotes is like a witness who tells you everything he knows, including what he is less sure of. Next, show him the scale. Such notes touch a small number of places, and the great passages he loves stand in all the manuscripts alike. Then show him the anchor this course has given you: no teaching of the faith rests on a disputed verse alone. Whatever the note concerns, the same truth is taught clearly in other places that every manuscript shares. Finally, bring him back to the Book in his hands. Do not leave him with a puzzle; leave him with a promise. God has kept His Word so richly and so openly that even its small uncertainties are printed in plain sight for every reader to see. A pastor who answers this way does more than calm one worried member. He shows the whole church that their faith does not need to be protected from the facts, because the facts serve the Word.

*(about 300 words)*

### 5. Unit 10, after [b020] — The Word Still Travelling: The Unfinished Task

**WHY:** The course ends by saying the authority of God "travels into your hands" through translation. It never mentions that for many peoples in Africa and the Americas that journey is not yet complete. Adding this would turn the course's conclusion into a call to mission, which fits pastors in those regions.

**PROPOSED TEXT:**

> **The Word Still Travelling: The Unfinished Task**
>
> We have said that the authority of God travels into the Bible in your hands. But we must also say an honest and sobering thing: for many peoples, the journey is not yet finished. More than seven thousand languages are spoken in the world today. By recent counts of Bible translation agencies, a little over seven hundred have the whole Bible, roughly seventeen hundred more have the New Testament, and many others have only a few books or none at all. Africa alone has more than two thousand languages. The Americas still have hundreds of indigenous languages, from Mexico to the Amazon to the Andes, where believers hear the Word only in a second language, or not at all. That is why the work of the translators did not end with Reina or Segond. Today it is carried on by national translators, many of them pastors and lay leaders from the very churches this seminary serves, working with Bible societies and translation agencies, checking every verse with care. Their aim is the vision of Revelation: "a great multitude which no one could number, of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). So the certainty you have received is not only a treasure to keep. It is a debt to pay. Pray for the translators. Support them if you can. If God has given you a second language, ask whether He might use it. "How shall they believe in Him of whom they have not heard?" (Romans 10:14). The Word that reached your hands is still travelling, and you may help carry it.

*(about 290 words)*

## Errors or problems found

1. **NKJV wording not used (guardrail).**
   - Unit 9 [b020]: "about threescore furlongs" (Luke 24:13) and "about fifteen furlongs" (John 11:18) are KJV wording. The NKJV reads "seven miles" and "about two miles away."
   - Unit 10 [b025] and [b033]: "Prove all things; hold fast that which is good" (1 Thess. 5:21) is the KJV. The NKJV reads "Test all things; hold fast what is good." Change this only if the exam does not quote the KJV form. If the exam does, keep it and mark it "(KJV)."

2. **Unit 9 [b016], Pool of Bethesda: factual error.** The text says the pool "had precisely that unusual five-sided colonnaded shape." The five porticoes were not a five-sided (pentagonal) shape. There were colonnades on the four sides and a fifth across the dividing wall between two basins. Suggested fix: "...and it had colonnades on its four sides and a fifth across the middle, between its two basins — exactly five."

3. **Unit 3 [b027], Apocrypha history: overstated.** The phrase "That lower tier held for a thousand years, until Trent" will not stand before an informed Catholic or Orthodox reader. Augustine accepted these books. The Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), which Unit 3 itself cites for the New Testament list ([b018]), included Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach and Maccabees in their Old Testament lists. The Council of Florence (1442) did as well. Also, the label "ecclesiastical" is more properly Rufinus's term. Jerome called these books "apocrypha," and his point was that the church reads them for edification, not to establish doctrine. Suggested fix: "Jerome... held them to be read for edification but not for establishing doctrine; many in the Western church followed him, though others, such as Augustine and the councils of Hippo and Carthage, received them more fully. Trent... made the fuller view dogma in 1546." This keeps the course's conclusion while being fair about the history.

4. **Unit 2 [b021], 22 vs. 24 count: incomplete.** The combinations listed (the Twelve as one book; Samuel, Kings, Chronicles and Ezra-Nehemiah each as one) produce 24. The count of 22 also joins Ruth to Judges and Lamentations to Jeremiah. Suggested fix: add "and, for the count of 22, Ruth with Judges and Lamentations with Jeremiah."

5. **Unit 2 [b013], minor.** "The Psalms, the first and largest book of the Writings": Psalms comes first in most printed Hebrew Bibles, but not in every ancient order (the Talmud puts Ruth first, and the Leningrad Codex puts Chronicles first). Suggest "the first (in most Hebrew Bibles) and best-known book of the Writings."

6. **Unit 4 [b018], Unit 4 [b024] and Unit 5 [b009], modest but dated figure.** "More than five thousand Greek manuscripts" is true but understated. The current catalogued figure is about 5,800 or more. Keep "more than five thousand" if the exam uses it. Otherwise "nearly six thousand" is more current.

7. **Unit 5 [b012], P52 dating: hedge slightly understated.** The lesson says "scholars still debate the exact decade." In fact some recent scholars (for example, Brent Nongbri) argue for a much wider range, from the second into the early third century. Suggest "scholars still debate its date, and some would place it later" to keep the claim modest. The argument against a date in the 160s for John still stands on other grounds.

8. **Unit 5 [b018] (and Unit 6 [b018]), minor.** The story that the Sinaiticus leaves were "nearly lost" or rescued "from a wastebasket" comes from Tischendorf's own account, and Saint Catherine's Monastery disputes it. In [b018] of Unit 6 the line is spoken in the Majority Text advocate's voice, which is acceptable. In Unit 5, consider "a story Tischendorf told of leaves nearly lost, though the monastery tells it differently."

9. **Unit 1 [b016] and Unit 10 [b018], small overgeneralization.** "The apostles preached... from a translation, the Greek Old Testament." They often did, but they also drew on the Hebrew. Suggest "often from a translation."

10. **Unit 3 [b027], minor.** "Jude alludes to Enoch": Jude 14–15 actually quotes 1 Enoch directly. "Jude quotes Enoch" is more accurate and makes the point (that quotation is not canonization) even stronger.
