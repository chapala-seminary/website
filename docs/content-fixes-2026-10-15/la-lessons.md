## 4. Unit 9, "Notice that our unit scripture…" paragraph (block b014)

**Before (English):**

> Notice that our unit scripture — Romans 1:16–17 — contains all four of the words we will study tonight. Paul packs them into two verses: sôtêria (salvation, the noun-form of sôzô), pisteuonti (believing, the verb-form of pistis), dikaiosynê, and pistis again. **After (English):**

> Notice that our unit scripture — Romans 1:16–17 — contains three of the four words we will study tonight. Paul packs them into two verses: sôtêria (salvation, the noun-form of sôzô), pisteuonti (believing, the verb-form of pistis), dikaiosynê, and pistis again. The fourth word, kyrios (Lord), is not in these two verses; we meet it in Philippians 2:9–11. The whole Reformation is hidden in two verses, and the key to the safe is three Greek words.

**Why:** Romans 1:16–17 does not contain all four words. The Greek has sôtêria (salvation, from the family of sôzô), pisteuonti and pistis (faith), and dikaiosynê, but not kyrios. The lesson itself (block b065) and the Unit 9 multiple-choice question 15 already say kyrios is absent, so b014 was the one that was wrong.

**Spanish and French:** changed to match.


## 5. Unit 9, Luther story (block b010)

**Before (English):**

> The Latin word in his Bible was justitia Dei, "the justice of God." To Luther, the righteousness of God meant the perfect standard by which God would judge him and find him guilty. The verse read like a death sentence. How could that be good news? He hated the verse. He hated God for putting it in the Bible. And then, while he was preparing his lectures, he opened the Greek text Erasmus had published just months earlier in 1516, and he read the same verse in the language Paul actually wrote: dikaiosynê theou. He looked at the verb forms. He saw that dikaiosynê in Paul did not mean the standard by which God judges. It meant the righteousness God gives as a free gift to those who believe. The just shall live by faith. Not by works. Not by climbing stairs on bloody knees. By faith.

**After (English):**

> The Latin word in his Bible was justitia Dei, "the justice of God." To Luther, the righteousness of God meant the perfect standard by which God would judge him and find him guilty. The verse read like a death sentence. How could that be good news? He hated the verse. He hated God for putting it in the Bible. And then, while he was still working through Romans in his lectures (1515–1516), he opened the Greek text Erasmus published in early 1516, and he read the same verse in the language Paul actually wrote: dikaiosynê theou. He looked at the verb forms. He saw that dikaiosynê in Paul did not mean the standard by which God judges. It meant the righteousness God gives as a free gift to those who believe. The just shall live by faith. Not by works. Not by climbing stairs on bloody knees. By faith.

**Why:** The lesson had Luther "preparing lectures" in 1515 and yet reading a Greek Testament "published just months earlier in 1516", which cannot both be true. Luther lectured on Romans from 1515 into 1516, and Erasmus's Greek New Testament came out in early 1516, during that course. The sentence now says exactly that and no more.

**Spanish and French:** changed to match.


## 6. Unit 9, second Luther paragraph (block b042)

**Before (English):**

> Now go back to Luther in his study. The Latin Bible he had used all his life translated dikaiosynê as justitia, "justice." But the Latin word, by the year 1515, had drifted to mean the punitive justice of a judge — the standard by which God condemns. When Luther opened Erasmus's freshly printed Greek New Testament in 1516 and read Romans 1:17, he saw something the Latin had hidden: dikaiosynê theou is not the standard God demands. It is the gift God gives. "The just shall live by faith" — ho dikaios ek pisteôs zêsetai. Luther later wrote: "I felt myself to be born anew and to enter through open gates into paradise itself." That sentence, in the tower at Wittenberg, was the spark of the Reformation. One Greek word. One reformer's understanding. Half a millennium of Protestant history.

**After (English):**

> Now go back to Luther in his study. The Latin Bible he had used all his life translated dikaiosynê as justitia, "justice." But the Latin word, by the year 1515, had drifted to mean the punitive justice of a judge — the standard by which God condemns. When Luther opened Erasmus's Greek New Testament, printed in 1516, and read Romans 1:17, he saw something the Latin had hidden: dikaiosynê theou is not the standard God demands. It is the gift God gives. "The just shall live by faith" — ho dikaios ek pisteôs zêsetai. Luther later wrote: "I felt myself to be born anew and to enter through open gates into paradise itself." That sentence, in the tower at Wittenberg, was the spark of the Reformation. One Greek word. One reformer's understanding. Half a millennium of Protestant history.

**Why:** Same chronology point. "Freshly printed" suggested Luther opened the book the moment it came out; the text now only says the book was printed in 1516.
