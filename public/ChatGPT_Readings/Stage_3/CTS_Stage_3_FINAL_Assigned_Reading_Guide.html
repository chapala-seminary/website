Old Testament Survey

1. The Divine Library of the Old Testament — A. F. Kirkpatrick (1891)

A. F. Kirkpatrick · 1891
Assigned portion: Introduction and opening chapter on the unity and structure of the Old Testament
Approximate length: 2,500 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Kirkpatrick's classic gives a clear and reverent overview of the Old Testament as a whole — how its books came to be, how they fit together, and what single great story they tell. It is an ideal companion to the whole course, standing back from the details to show the shape and message of the entire collection. For the student beginning a survey, it answers the first and most important questions: what is the Old Testament, how did God give it, and how are its many parts bound into one unfolding revelation leading on to Christ.

2. Bible History: Old Testament — Alfred Edersheim

Alfred Edersheim
Assigned portion: Preface and opening section on creation and the earliest biblical history
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Edersheim's beloved history retells the story of the Old Testament from creation to the return from exile with warmth, learning, and a constant eye to its meaning. Written by a Hebrew Christian of deep scholarship, it makes the whole sweep of Israel's history live — the patriarchs, the exodus, the judges, the kings, the prophets, and the captivity. It richly serves the course's historical units, and is perhaps the finest single guide for reading the Old Testament as one continuous, God-directed story moving steadily toward the coming of the Messiah.

3. David: Shepherd, Psalmist, King — F. B. Meyer

F. B. Meyer
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on David’s youth and calling
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Meyer's devotional life of David walks from the sheepfold to the throne — the shepherd boy, the giant-killer, the hunted fugitive, the king after God's own heart, and the sweet singer of Israel. It brings the course's unit on the united kingdom to life, and opens the heart of the man whose story fills so much of the Old Testament. Accessible and warmly spiritual, it draws from David's triumphs and failures alike rich lessons on faith, repentance, and the faithfulness of God — and points to David's greater Son, the King whose throne shall never end.

4. The Book of Job — A. B. Davidson (1884)

A. B. Davidson · 1884
Assigned portion: Introduction and opening discussion of the book’s problem
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Davidson's careful commentary opens the Book of Job — the great Old Testament wrestling with the mystery of suffering, and with the ways of God toward those who fear Him. Clear and thoughtful, it guides the reader through the speeches and the storm to the book's humbling close, where Job meets God Himself and is satisfied. It serves the course's unit on the wisdom literature, and helps the student see that Job is no mere ancient debate, but a book that touches the deepest questions of the human heart — and answers them, not with an argument, but with God.

5. Laws from Heaven for Life on Earth (Proverbs) — William Arnot (1857)

William Arnot · 1857
Assigned portion: Introduction and first exposition from Proverbs
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Arnot's warm and practical work opens the Book of Proverbs — the treasury of God's wisdom for daily living. Taking up its sayings one by one, he draws out their meaning with vivid illustration and homely good sense, showing that true wisdom begins with the fear of the Lord. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the wisdom books, making Proverbs not a scattered collection of maxims but a coherent school of godly living. Read it to learn the heavenly laws that make for a wise, upright, and useful life on earth.

6. The Book of Isaiah — George L. Robinson (1910)

George L. Robinson · 1910
Assigned portion: Introduction and opening section on Isaiah’s historical setting
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Robinson's accessible study opens Isaiah, the greatest of the prophets — his times, his message, and his soaring vision of the Holy One of Israel and the Servant who would bear the sins of many. It serves the course's unit on the Major Prophets, guiding the reader through a long and rich book with a sure and steady hand. Above all it keeps in view what makes Isaiah so precious to the church: that here, centuries beforehand, the coming, the suffering, and the glory of Christ are painted in words the Gospels themselves would take up.

7. The Twelve Minor Prophets — George L. Robinson (1907)

George L. Robinson · 1907
Assigned portion: Introduction and opening treatment of Hosea
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Robinson's companion volume introduces the twelve Minor Prophets — short books, but weighty with the word of the Lord. Book by book he sets each prophet in his time and draws out his message, from Hosea's wounded love to Malachi's closing call. It serves the course's unit on the Minor Prophets, rescuing these often-neglected books from obscurity. Read it to hear their united cry for justice, faithfulness, and repentance, and their steady hope of the day of the Lord and the Redeemer who was to come.

8. Rays of Messiah's Glory: Christ in the Old Testament — David Baron (1886)

David Baron · 1886
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on the Messiah in Old Testament promise
Approximate length: 2,500 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Baron, a Hebrew Christian of great learning, gathers the many prophecies and pictures of the Messiah scattered throughout the Old Testament and shows how they all find their fulfillment in Jesus. It serves the whole course, and especially its units on the prophets, revealing the golden thread that runs from Genesis to Malachi. Written by one who knew the Hebrew Scriptures from within, it is a moving demonstration that the Old Testament is not a closed book of a bygone people, but a long and glorious preparation for Christ — every ray of its light bending toward Him.

9. The Background of the Gospels — William Fairweather

William Fairweather
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on the Jewish and Greco-Roman setting
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Fairweather's study fills the long silence between the Testaments — the centuries from the return under Ezra and Nehemiah to the birth of Christ, when the world of the Gospels was being shaped. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the intertestamental period, describing the rise of the synagogue, the parties of the Jews, the spread of Greek and Roman power, and the deepening hope for a Deliverer. Read it to understand the stage into which Christ was born, and to see that even in the years when no prophet spoke, God was quietly preparing the fullness of time.

10. Synthetic Bible Studies — James M. Gray

James M. Gray
Assigned portion: Old Testament overview and the opening book-study section
Approximate length: 2,500 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Gray's practical guide teaches the student to survey each book of the Bible as a whole — to grasp its theme, its structure, and its message before diving into the parts. Covering the entire Scripture, its Old Testament portion is a valuable companion to this course, giving a method for mastering book after book rather than losing the forest for the trees. Read it to gain not only knowledge of the Old Testament but a skill for a lifetime: the ability to open any book of the Bible and see, at a glance, the shape of what God has said.

New Testament Survey

1. Introduction to the New Testament (modern)

J. Hampton Keathley III, Th.M. · bible.org · 2004
Assigned portion: The complete introductory article
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Keathley opens exactly where this unit opens — “when the fulness of the time was come” — and then answers the question that verse raises: why that moment? His answer comes in three preparations. The Jewish nation , kept as custodians of the oracles of God and the channel of the Redeemer, brought back from captivity with a remnant still watching for Messiah. The Greek language , left behind by Alexander as a world speech, a matchless vehicle of precision ready to carry the gospel to every province. And Roman rule , with its roads, its army, and its peace, making travel safe for the men who would carry it. He then lays out the book itself: twenty-seven books, nine authors, three classes — historical, epistolary, prophetical. Note the shape of it: an interrogative, a keyword, three parallel divisions. It is the cleanest map of this unit’s ground you will find in one sitting.

2. The Training of the Twelve — The Selection of the Twelve (classic)

A. B. Bruce · 1871 · chapter
Assigned portion: Chapter on the selection of the Twelve
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Bruce takes the moment Jesus chose twelve men out of the crowd of disciples and shows why it is a landmark, not a detail. It divides the ministry in two: before it, our Lord labored single-handed among a limited people with elementary teaching; after it, the work had grown so large it required organization and division of labor. Bruce’s point is bracing for any pastor — the selection became a necessity to Christ precisely because of His success. The crowds had become an impediment. So He called twelve “that they should be with Him” — being before doing, presence before assignment. Read it for the discipline of it: Christ built men before He built a movement.

3. Expository Thoughts on the Gospels: St. Mark (classic)

J. C. Ryle · 1859 · Ipswich: W. Hunt · opening chapters
Assigned portion: Preface and opening exposition of Mark 1
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Ryle described Mark as “singularly full of precious facts about the Lord Jesus, narrated in a simple, terse, pithy, and condensed style” — and the same four words describe Ryle. This is the right companion for a unit built on Mark’s action : no long discourses, no speculation, just the Servant moving. Ryle wrote for family prayers and for the sick-room, not for the lecture hall, so every paragraph lands somewhere useful. Take the opening chapters — the forerunner, the baptism, the calling of the first disciples, the first day in Capernaum — and watch how quickly he moves from the text to the conscience. He is the model of what this course means by plain preaching.

4. The Gospel of Luke: An Exposition — Foreword and Preface (classic)

Charles R. Erdman · 1921 · Westminster Press
Assigned portion: Foreword, preface, and opening exposition
Approximate length: 2,400 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Erdman begins by calling Luke the most beautiful book in the world, and then explains why the beauty is not decoration but doctrine. Luke was “the beloved physician” — a man of culture whose Greek is the most finished in the New Testament, and a man of wide and tender sympathies whose narrative is suffused with emotion, full of gladness and sorrow, of songs and of tears. It is the gospel of childhood; it alone preserves the boyhood of our Lord. That is precisely this unit’s theme: the compassion of the Son of Man is not a mood Luke adds, it is the lens God gave him. Read the Foreword and the exposition of the Preface (Luke 1:1–4) together — short, and it will change how you read the rest.

5. The Life of St. Paul — Chapter IX: His Great Controversy (classic)

James Stalker · 1884 · chapter IX of X
Assigned portion: Chapter IX, “His Great Controversy”
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Stalker’s little book is still the best brief life of Paul in English, and chapter IX is the one this unit needs. The Great Controversy is the Judaizing crisis — whether a Gentile must become a Jew before he may become a Christian — and it is the fight that produced Galatians and shaped Romans. Stalker tells it as a story with a doctrine inside it, which is why it works where a commentary would not: you feel what was at stake before you are asked to parse it. He is also honest that Paul stood nearly alone. Read it before Galatians and the epistle stops being an argument and becomes a rescue.

6. The Second Epistle to the Corinthians — Opening Chapters (classic)

James Denney · 1894 · The Expositor’s Bible
Assigned portion: Introduction and opening exposition of 2 Corinthians 1
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Second Corinthians is the most personal thing Paul wrote, and Denney is the expositor who best feels it. He opens on the new name Paul found for God in the worst of his troubles — “the Father of mercies and God of all comfort” — and makes a point every pastor needs: God was never so much to Paul as when he was in the sorest straits. Denney is careful, too, about a real abuse. Not every affliction is a share in the sufferings of Christ; this comfort is promised to those who suffer for following Him , and a minister is not at liberty to apply it to any trouble he meets. Sober, warm, and exactly the register this course wants.

7. The Prison Epistles (modern)

Dr. J. Vernon McGee · Blue Letter Bible · notes & outlines
Assigned portion: Introductory notes and outline for Ephesians
Approximate length: 2,300 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

A quartet of men left Rome in A.D. 62 carrying four of the most sublime compositions in the Christian faith, and Rome never knew what was in their hands. McGee names each courier — Epaphroditus, Tychicus, Epaphras, and Onesimus, a runaway slave carrying the letter about himself — and then does something better than a summary. He sets the four letters as one figure. Ephesians : Christ the center, the church the circumference — “the church, which is his body.” Colossians : Christ the circumference, Christian living the center. Philippians : Christ the center, Christian living the circumference. Philemon : Christ both center and circumference — the gospel in shoe leather, and it worked. Read it in ten minutes; you will keep the shape for years.

8. The Epistles of St. Peter — Preface and Opening (classic)

J. Rawson Lumby · 1893 · The Expositor’s Bible
Assigned portion: Preface and opening exposition of 1 Peter 1
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Peter writes to strangers and pilgrims under fire, which is exactly what this unit means by faith tested in life. Lumby is useful here for two reasons. First, he handles the parallels between Peter and James the way a pastor should: the resemblances prove only that the apostolic missionaries taught a common message to people facing common dangers — they are no proof that one copied the other, whatever the critics say. Second, he keeps the letters where they belong, in the hands of ordinary believers being told to gird up the loins of the mind and lay aside malice. A steady, believing guide through the first of the General Epistles.

9. The Epistles of St. John — The Apostle and His Letters (classic)

William Alexander · 1889 · The Expositor’s Bible
Assigned portion: Introduction: the apostle and his letters
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Alexander opens by drawing John out of the closing chapter of the fourth Gospel, through the contrast with Peter — two apostles whose work for the church differed as sharply as their characters did. You might have expected the disciple whom Jesus loved to become the great missionary to the nations; he did not. Alexander is worth reading for the discipline he shows as well as the content: he lists the commentators he studied for years — Augustine, Chrysostom, Bengel above all — and then says he now sits down with nothing before him but a Greek Testament. That is a model of how a man earns the right to speak plainly. Pair it with Lumby and the General Epistles come into focus from two sides.

10. The Book of Revelation — The Vision of Chapter One (classic)

William Milligan · 1889 · The Expositor’s Bible
Assigned portion: Introduction and exposition of Revelation 1
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Take this one for the vision, not for a timetable. Milligan’s opening chapter stops at the first vision — the glorified Son of man walking among the lampstands — and says rightly that it contains the key-note of the whole book. Where the fourth Gospel shows the Word made flesh pre-eminently as Son of God, the Apocalypse shows the same Lord pre-eminently as Son of man: the Redeemer who finished His work on earth and carries it on in heaven, now Head over all things to His church and clothed with judgment. Milligan also names who this book was written for — those who feel the scorn and hatred of the world — and observes that its revelations, like the stars, shine brightest in the cold, dark night. Note well: Milligan reads the Apocalypse spiritually, and CTS does not take a millennial side. Your unit presents the premillennial, postmillennial, and amillennial positions even-handedly, and the interpretive approaches with them; this reading is offered for its Christology, and his framework is his own, not the seminary’s.

Pentateuch

1. Biblical Commentary on the Old Testament: The Pentateuch — Introduction (classic)

C. F. Keil · 1864–85 · T. & T. Clark · vol. 1 (the Law)
Assigned portion: Introduction to the Pentateuch
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Keil opens where this unit opens, and he does not hedge: “The five books of Moses.” He works through what the Pentateuch calls itself — Sepher hattorah , the Law-book; or simply Hattorah , the Law — and shows that the name describes both its contents and its standing in the Old Covenant. This is the best single entry into the whole course: the five books as one book, with one Author and one Lawgiver. Keil is the conservative half of “Keil & Delitzsch” and the Pentateuch volumes are his; read him for the ground the other four units stand on.

2. Gleanings in Genesis — “Two Trees” (Genesis 2) (classic)

Arthur W. Pink · 1922 · chapter 3 of ~50
Assigned portion: Chapter “Two Trees” on Genesis 2
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Pink sets the tree in Eden beside the tree at Calvary and will not let you look at one without the other. Genesis 3:6 said the first tree was good for food , pleasant to the eyes , and to be desired to make one wise — and Pink takes each of the three and shows the Cross answering it: good for food, for it is the meat and marrow of the believer’s life; pleasant to the eyes of faith, for there we see our sins blotted out; and desired to make one wise, for the preaching of the Cross is the wisdom of God. Note the shape — one text, three parallel divisions, a single keyword. Note: Pink writes from a Reformed position; this chapter carries none of it.

3. The Pentateuch — Genesis: The Flood and the Nations (classic)

C. F. Keil · 1864–85 · vol. 1, on Genesis 6–11
Assigned portion: Commentary on Genesis 6–11: the Flood and the nations
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Keil takes the Flood and Babel as history, and the care he spends on the detail is the argument. He works the chronology, the geography, and the table of nations as a man handling a record rather than a legend — which is precisely what this unit needs, because Genesis 6–11 is where the modern reader is most often told to stop believing. Keil grants nothing and explains everything. Read it alongside Unit 3 and notice how much of the “problem” dissolves when the text is read carefully instead of defensively. This is Keil’s second appearance on this shelf — a different book of the Pentateuch, a different question.

4. Gleanings in Genesis — “The Call of Abraham” (Genesis 12) (classic)

Arthur W. Pink · 1922 · chapter 17 of ~50
Assigned portion: Chapter on Genesis 12 and Abraham’s call
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

The call came to Abram in Mesopotamia and it demanded two things: confidence in the word of Jehovah, and separation from everything natural. Pink is honest about what happened next — Abram rendered a tardy and partial obedience . He took his father and his nephew; he stopped at Haran and stayed there till Terah died. And there is no record of God speaking again until the call had been fully obeyed. Then Pink turns it: when Hebrews 11 recalls this, Abram’s failure is not mentioned — “By faith Abram… obeyed.” That sin, with all his others, had been blotted out. First the obedience of faith, then communion and worship. There was no altar for Abram in Ur or Haran.

5. The Book of Exodus — Preface and Opening (classic)

G. A. Chadwick, Dean of Armagh · 1890 · The Expositor’s Bible
Assigned portion: Preface, introduction, and opening exposition
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Chadwick begins by conceding the ground and then taking it back in one sentence. Much about Exodus is now denied or doubted inside the church, he grants — but one thing can neither be doubted nor denied: Jesus Christ did certainly treat this book, taking it as He found it, as possessed of spiritual authority. He taught His disciples to regard it so, and they did. That is the whole case for a high view of Exodus, and Chadwick rests it on Christ rather than on argument. He is equally firm the other way: he refuses to let imagination do the work of reason in sacred things, because no honour is done to the Spirit of knowledge and wisdom by fancy and quaint conceits . A guard worth having before Units 7 and 11.

6. The Tabernacle, the Priesthood, and the Offerings — The Vail and the Way In (classic)

Henry W. Soltau · 1851 · 474 pp with 21 plates
Assigned portion: Section “The Vail and the Way In”
Approximate length: 2,500 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Soltau gave up a Chancery practice to study this, and it shows — he measures the tabernacle the way a barrister reads a deed, and every measurement preaches. Take his treatment of the vail and the way into the holiest: access is in the blood , so the way is not merely opened but the worshipper himself is fitted to walk it; and though there can be only one Great High Priest, believers who constitute the royal priesthood share His nearness of approach. He notices that the vail’s four pillars had no capitals — they lacked the ordinary completeness of a pillar — and asks what that may be telling us. Note: Soltau writes from the Brethren tradition; expect dispensational framing, and read the types as illustration rather than proof.

7. The Book of Leviticus — Introduction and the Offerings (classic)

S. H. Kellogg · 1891 · The Expositor’s Bible
Assigned portion: Introduction and opening treatment of the offerings
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Kellogg is the reason this shelf can be trusted on the Law. He states plainly what the Jews of our Lord’s day believed about the Mosaic origin of this book, that no second opinion is traceable before the Christian era — and that Jesus Christ shared and repeatedly endorsed that belief, calling it the law of Moses and declaring that not one jot or tittle should pass till all be fulfilled. Then he opens the offerings, and the whole Levitical ritual turns out to be the most striking illustration in Scripture of a Lamb foreknown before the foundation of the world. Note where he begins: at the tent of meeting, where the first words from Sinai had been law and the first words here are grace.

8. The Book of Numbers — Opening: The Book as Drama (classic)

R. A. Watson · 1889 · The Expositor’s Bible
Assigned portion: Introduction and opening section, “The Book as Drama”
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Watson opens Numbers by comparing it to Greek drama, and the comparison earns itself: a main idea, groups of persons carrying it out or opposing it, and a keen ethical strain that makes the movement tragic throughout. Moses stands at the centre with his helpers and his opponents — Aaron and Miriam, Joshua and Hobab, Korah, Dathan and Abiram, Balak and Balaam. He is brought to extremity; he despairs and appeals to Heaven; and in one hour of pride he falls into the sin that brings doom on him. Watson calls it eternal literature more fitly than the Iliad has been called an eternal poem. For a unit about wandering, this is the reading that shows you the wandering had a plot. Take the opening; his later section on priests and Levites raises critical questions it does not settle.

9. The Typology of Scripture — The Principles of Type (classic)

Patrick Fairbairn · 1845 · vol. 1 of 2
Assigned portion: Introduction and principles governing biblical types
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Every reader of the Pentateuch eventually asks how far a type may be pressed, and Fairbairn is the man who answered it. He viewed typology in connection with the whole series of the divine dispensations — which is his way of saying that a type is not a riddle planted for clever readers, but a real thing in a real history that God shaped toward Christ. That rule cuts both ways, and Fairbairn uses it both ways: it licenses the tabernacle and the offerings, and it forbids the fanciful. Read him before Soltau and Unit 11, not after; he supplies the discipline that keeps typology from becoming an imagination game. Note: Fairbairn writes from the Reformed tradition; this is a book about types, not about election.

10. Biblical Commentary on the Old Testament: Deuteronomy — Introduction (classic)

C. F. Keil and F. Delitzsch · 1866
Assigned portion: Introduction to Deuteronomy
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

The shelf already follows Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers, but it needs Deuteronomy to complete the five books of Moses. Keil introduces Deuteronomy as Moses’ final covenant address to Israel on the plains of Moab: not a second or rival law, but the law preached, explained, and pressed upon a new generation about to enter the land. This selection helps the reader see the book’s unity, its pastoral urgency, and its repeated summons to remember, love, obey, and teach the words of the LORD. Keil writes from a conservative Lutheran tradition and strongly defends the book’s Mosaic setting and essential unity.

Psalms

1. The Treasury of David — Charles H. Spurgeon

Charles H. Spurgeon
Assigned portion: Spurgeon’s exposition of Psalm 23
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Spurgeon labored for twenty years on The Treasury of David, and the result is perhaps the greatest devotional commentary on the Psalms ever written. For every Psalm he gives his own rich exposition, a treasury of quotations from other writers, and pointed hints for teaching. Warm, quotable, and endlessly rewarding, it is the ideal companion to the whole course — a lifetime's resource for understanding, loving, and preaching the Psalms. Whatever Psalm you are studying, begin here: you will rise from it with your mind instructed and your heart set singing.

2. Commentary on the Psalms — Franz Delitzsch

Franz Delitzsch
Assigned portion: Introduction and commentary on Psalm 1
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Delitzsch's commentary is the great scholarly guide to the Psalms, treating the Hebrew text with unmatched care and deep reverence. Where Spurgeon warms the heart, Delitzsch informs the mind, opening the meaning of the words, the poetry, and the structure of each Psalm with the fruit of a lifetime's learning. This is a technical work, offered for the student who wishes to go deep; but for the serious question of what a Psalm actually says, it remains, after more than a century, among the surest and most trustworthy guides in any language.

3. Sermons on Psalm 119 — Thomas Manton

Thomas Manton
Assigned portion: Opening sermon on Psalm 119:1
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Thomas Manton, a master among the Puritans, preached one hundred and ninety sermons on Psalm 119 — the longest Psalm, and the Bible's great song in praise of the Word of God. Verse by verse he unfolds the believer's love for God's law, its comfort in affliction, its light for the path, and its power to keep the heart. It is the perfect companion to the course's unit on Psalm 119. Vast though it is, the reader may take it in portions and find, in almost every page, a fresh reason to love and obey the Scriptures more.

4. The Lord Our Shepherd (Psalm 23) — John Stevenson (1845)

John Stevenson · 1845
Assigned portion: Opening section on Psalm 23
Approximate length: 2,500 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Stevenson devotes an entire book to the best-loved of all the Psalms, unfolding the Twenty-third Psalm phrase by phrase — the Shepherd who provides, leads, restores, and stays beside His sheep through the valley of the shadow. Tender and rich, it draws out depths in a Psalm so familiar we may cease to feel it. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the Shepherd Psalm, and a comfort in itself. Read it slowly, and this ancient song of trust will do its quiet work: to still the anxious heart with the goodness and mercy that follow us all our days.

5. Christ on the Cross (Psalm 22) — John Stevenson (1842)

John Stevenson · 1842
Assigned portion: Opening exposition of Psalm 22
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

In this companion volume Stevenson expounds Psalm 22 — the Psalm that opens with the very cry Christ uttered on the cross, and paints His sufferings a thousand years before they came to pass. Phrase by phrase he shows how the pierced hands, the mocking crowd, and the parted garments were all foretold here, and how the Psalm turns at last from anguish to triumph. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the Messianic Psalm, leading the reader to Calvary through the words of David, and to a deeper wonder at the Savior who bore the forsaking that we might never be forsaken.

6. The Doctrine of Repentance — Thomas Watson (1668)

Thomas Watson · 1668
Assigned portion: Opening chapters defining true repentance
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Watson's Puritan classic goes to the heart of Psalm 51 — the broken and contrite spirit that God will not despise. With searching clarity he anatomizes true repentance: what it is, its marks, and how it differs from mere regret, calling the reader from a shallow sorrow to the godly grief that turns wholly to God. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the penitential Psalm. Read it to feel the weight and the sweetness of true repentance — and to make David's prayer your own: ‘Create in me a clean heart, O God.’

7. The Psalms (Expositor's Bible) — Alexander Maclaren

Alexander Maclaren
Assigned portion: Introduction and exposition of Psalm 1
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Maclaren's exposition of the Psalms brings the touch of a great preacher to the whole book, opening one Psalm after another with insight, beauty, and warmth. 8. Letter to Marcellinus on the Psalms — Athanasius

Athanasius
Assigned portion: The complete Letter to Marcellinus on the Psalms
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

In this ancient and beautiful letter, Athanasius — one of the great fathers of the early church — explains how the Psalms are unlike any other part of Scripture: for in them we do not only read of the feelings of the soul, but find our own put into words, and given back to us as prayer. He shows which Psalms fit which occasions — for joy and for grief, for fear and for thanksgiving. It is a fitting companion to the course's units on the Psalms in ministry, and among the wisest short guides ever written for making the whole Psalter one's own daily prayer.

Rowland E. Prothero · 1903

Why this reading matters

Prothero's remarkable book tells the story of what the Psalms have meant to men and women across the centuries — the words that soldiers sang, that martyrs died with on their lips, that comforted kings and peasants, the dying and the grieving, in every age of the church. 1. The Acts of the Apostles (Commentary) — Joseph Addison Alexander (1857)

Joseph Addison Alexander · 1857
Assigned portion: Introduction and commentary on Acts 1
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only, not the entire book.
Why this reading matters

Alexander's commentary is one of the great nineteenth-century guides to Acts — careful, thorough, and conservative, treating the text with close attention and sound judgment. Verse by verse he explains the meaning, resolves difficulties, and traces the argument of Luke's narrative, always with reverence for the inspired record. Fuller and more demanding than a devotional aid, it is the ideal companion for the student who wants to understand Acts accurately and in depth. For a trustworthy exposition of what Luke actually wrote, it remains, after more than a century and a half, among the surest.

Ramsay set out as a skeptic to test the accuracy of Acts, and was won over by the evidence to become one of its greatest defenders. Allen's influential little book asks a searching question: how did Paul, in about ten years, plant churches across whole provinces — and why do our methods look so different?
