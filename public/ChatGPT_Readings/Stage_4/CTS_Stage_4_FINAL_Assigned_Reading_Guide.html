CTS Stage 4 — FINAL Assigned Reading Guide

Matthew · John · Romans · Galatians · Revelation

Student instruction: Read only the assigned chapter, passage, or excerpt. The readings supplement lectures and examinations; they do not replace course instruction.

Matthew

1. Expository Thoughts on the Gospels: Matthew — J. C. Ryle

J. C. Ryle
Assigned portion: Matthew 5:1–16, Ryle’s exposition
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Ryle's Expository Thoughts on Matthew bring the same plain, warm, heart-searching help to the first Gospel that generations have loved in his other volumes. Passage by passage he explains the text simply and drives its lessons home, never losing sight of the living Christ or the reader's soul. Practical, evangelical, and endlessly useful, it is the ideal everyday companion to the course — equally at home in the hand of a new believer and a seasoned preacher. Read it alongside your study of each unit, and you will find Matthew's King made near, and His claims pressed gently but firmly on the conscience.

2. Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew — John A. Broadus (1886)

John A. Broadus · 1886
Assigned portion: Introduction and commentary on Matthew 5:1–12
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Broadus was one of the finest New Testament scholars of his century, and his commentary on Matthew joins careful learning to warm faith and clear expression. He attends to the meaning of the Greek, the setting of each passage, and the difficulties that arise, always with a view to helping the teacher and preacher. Fuller and more detailed than a devotional guide, it is the ideal companion for the student who wants to understand Matthew thoroughly and accurately. For a trustworthy, believing, and richly informed exposition of the first Gospel, it has few equals among works of its kind.

3. The Gospel of the Kingdom — Charles H. Spurgeon

Charles H. Spurgeon
Assigned portion: Exposition of Matthew 5:1–16
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

The Gospel of the Kingdom was the last work of Spurgeon's life — a warm, popular commentary on the whole of Matthew, written as he was dying, out of a lifetime of loving the King. Brief and pointed, it opens each passage with the freshness and spiritual power for which he was famous, always aiming at the heart and the will. It is a perfect companion to the course, suited to every reader, and a fitting testament from the prince of preachers. Read it to feel the pulse of Matthew's great theme — the kingdom of heaven, and the King who bids us enter and follow Him.

4. An Exposition of the Sermon on the Mount — Arthur W. Pink

Arthur W. Pink
Assigned portion: Opening chapter and exposition of Matthew 5:1–3
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Pink's thorough exposition opens the Sermon on the Mount — the great charter of the kingdom, in which the King sets forth the character, righteousness, and life of His people. Verse by verse he unfolds the Beatitudes, the teaching on the law, prayer, and the two ways, showing that these are not counsels of mere outward conduct but the description of a heart renewed by grace. It richly serves the course's units on the Sermon. Read it to be searched and humbled by the King's own words, and to see how high, and how inward, is the righteousness of His kingdom.

5. The Beatitudes — Thomas Watson

Thomas Watson
Assigned portion: Opening exposition of Matthew 5:1–3
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Watson's classic exposition of the Beatitudes opens the opening words of the Sermon on the Mount — the King's portrait of the citizen of His kingdom: poor in spirit, mourning, meek, hungering for righteousness, merciful, pure in heart, a peacemaker. With the searching practicality of the best Puritan writers, he shows what each blessing means and how it is found. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the character of the kingdom. Read it slowly, testing your heart by each mark, and let the King's blesseds both humble you and draw you up toward the life that is truly blessed.

6. Notes on the Parables of Our Lord — Richard C. Trench

Richard C. Trench
Assigned portion: Introduction and the parable of the sower
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Trench's Notes on the Parables is the classic English work on our Lord's parables, and no better guide has been written to the parables of the kingdom that fill Matthew's Gospel. With learning and reverence he expounds each one — the sower, the tares, the treasure, the pearl, the net, and the rest — drawing out its meaning and guarding against false interpretations. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the parables. Read it to learn how to read a parable rightly, and to enter more deeply into the mysteries of the kingdom that the King unfolded in these unforgettable stories.

7. Expository Thoughts on Matthew 28:16–20 — J. C. Ryle

Richard C. Trench
Assigned portion: Matthew 28:16–20 (The Great Commission)
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

In this companion volume Trench treats the miracles of Christ with the same care and depth he brought to the parables. He shows that the miracles were not mere wonders but signs — displays of the King's authority over disease, demons, nature, and death, and pictures of His power to save. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the King's authority. Read it to see the miracles of Matthew's Gospel not as isolated marvels but as the credentials of the King and the tokens of His kingdom, in which all that sin has broken shall at last be made whole.

8. The Shadow of Calvary — Hugh Martin

Hugh Martin
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on Christ’s approach to the cross
Approximate length: 2,500 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Martin's profound work leads the reader into the last hours before the cross — Gethsemane, the betrayal, and the trial — that Matthew records with such solemnity. With rare spiritual depth he unfolds the agony in the garden and the sufferings of the sinless One as He drew near to bear the sin of the world. It is a fitting companion to the course's unit on the passion. Read it reverently, and slowly; it will lead you into the shadow of Calvary, to wonder at the love and the anguish of the King who set His face to die, that His people might live.

9. The Lord of Glory — B. B. Warfield

B. B. Warfield
Assigned portion: Essay section on the divine glory of Christ
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Warfield's careful study gathers the testimony of the Gospels to the deity of Christ — the truth, woven through Matthew from the virgin birth to ‘all authority in heaven and on earth,’ that the King is Himself divine, God the Son come in the flesh. With patience and force he shows how the Gospel writers, and Jesus Himself, bear witness to this. It is a valuable companion to the course's units on the person of the King. Read it to see that Matthew's Jesus is no mere teacher or prophet, but the Lord of glory — worthy of the worship the wise men gave Him, and of ours.

10. Prophecy and History in Relation to the Messiah — Alfred Edersheim

Alfred Edersheim
Assigned portion: Opening lecture on messianic prophecy
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Edersheim, a Hebrew Christian of vast learning, traces how the hope of the Messiah runs through the whole Old Testament and the history of Israel, coming at last to fulfillment in Jesus. It illuminates the deepest concern of Matthew's Gospel, which again and again says of Christ, ‘that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet.’ It is a valuable companion to the course's opening units on the King's origins. Read it to see, as Matthew's first readers were meant to see, that Jesus is no afterthought but the promised One toward whom all the centuries were leaning — the King foretold, and at last revealed.

John

1. Expository Thoughts on the Gospels: John — J. C. Ryle

J. C. Ryle
Assigned portion: John 1:1–18, Ryle’s exposition
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Ryle's Expository Thoughts on John are among the most treasured aids to the Gospel ever written — plain, warm, deeply practical, and always aimed at the heart. Passage by passage he explains the text simply, presses its lessons home, and gathers gems from the older writers. He never loses the wood for the trees, holding always before the reader the living Christ of the Gospel and the soul's need to believe on Him. It is the ideal everyday companion to the course: accessible to every reader, rich enough for the scholar, and warm enough to send you back to Christ Himself.

2. Commentary on the Gospel of John — Frédéric Godet

Frédéric Godet
Assigned portion: Introduction and commentary on John 1:1–18
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Godet's commentary is among the finest scholarly guides to John ever written — the fruit of vast learning joined to a warm evangelical faith. He treats the Greek text and the flow of the Gospel with great care, defending its truth and unfolding its meaning against the doubts of his day, yet never coldly: for Godet loved the Christ he studied. This is a fuller and more demanding work, offered for the student who wishes to go deep. For a trustworthy, believing, and thorough exposition of what John actually wrote and meant, it remains a standard after well over a century.

3. Exposition of the Gospel of John — Arthur W. Pink

Arthur W. Pink
Assigned portion: John 1:1–18
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Pink's exposition is a monumental verse-by-verse study of John, gathering the doctrinal and spiritual riches of the Gospel with great thoroughness. He lingers over the meaning of each passage, drawing out its teaching about the person and work of Christ and its application to the believer's life. Substantial and rewarding, it is for the reader who wishes to work through the Gospel slowly and deeply. Whatever scene the course sets before you — the new birth, the bread of life, the good shepherd — Pink will help you mine it, and rise from the study with a larger view of the Savior.

4. On the Incarnation — Athanasius

Athanasius
Assigned portion: Sections 1–10
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Written sixteen centuries ago by one of the greatest of the church fathers, On the Incarnation is the classic treatment of the truth with which John's Gospel opens: that the eternal Word became flesh and dwelt among us. With clarity and wonder Athanasius explains why the Son of God took our nature, and how by His death and rising He conquered death and restored us to God. It is the perfect companion to the course's first unit, the Word made flesh. Short, profound, and thrilling, it shows that the deepest thinking of the church has always begun where John begins — at the manger and the mystery of God with us.

5. Our Lord Prays for His Own (John 17) — Marcus Rainsford (1873)

Marcus Rainsford · 1873
Assigned portion: Opening exposition of John 17:1–5
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Rainsford devotes an entire book to the seventeenth chapter of John — the high-priestly prayer, in which our Lord, on the night before His death, prayed for His disciples and for all who would believe through their word. Reverent and rich, it leads the reader phrase by phrase through this holiest of prayers, showing what Christ asks for His people: their keeping, their unity, their joy, their holiness, and their being with Him to behold His glory. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on the prayer, and a means of grace in itself — for to read it is to overhear the Savior praying for you.

6. The True Vine (John 15) — Andrew Murray

Andrew Murray
Assigned portion: Chapters 1–3 on John 15:1–5
Approximate length: 2,500 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

In this short and beautiful devotional, Andrew Murray meditates on our Lord's words in John 15: ‘I am the true vine.’ In brief daily readings he unfolds the secret of the fruitful Christian life — not striving in our own strength, but abiding in Christ as the branch abides in the vine, and letting His life flow through us. It is a fitting companion to the course's unit on the true vine, and among the tenderest guides ever written to the life of communion with Christ. Read it slowly, a portion at a time, and let its one great lesson sink deep: ‘Abide in me, and I in you.’

7. The Trial and Death of Jesus Christ — James Stalker

James Stalker
Assigned portion: Chapter on the trial before Pilate
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Stalker's vivid classic follows our Lord through the last hours of His life — the arrest in the garden, the trials before the priests and Pilate, and the crucifixion. Written with the eye of a historian and the heart of a believer, it makes the scenes of John's closing chapters live again, and draws from each a searching spiritual lesson. It is an ideal companion to the course's unit on the passion. Read it to walk with Christ to the cross, to feel the weight of what He bore, and to understand the meaning of His last great cry: ‘It is finished.’

8. The Resurrection of Our Lord — William Milligan (1881)

William Milligan · 1881
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on the resurrection narratives
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Milligan's classic study takes up the crowning fact of the Gospel — the resurrection of Jesus from the dead. He weighs the evidence for it, expounds the New Testament accounts, and unfolds its meaning for the believer and for the world. It richly serves the course's units on the raising of Lazarus and the risen Lord, tracing the theme of resurrection life that runs through John from ‘I am the resurrection and the life’ to the empty tomb. Read it to stand on the sure ground of Easter — to know that Christ is risen indeed, and that because He lives, His people shall live also.

9. The Fountain of Life — John Flavel

John Flavel
Assigned portion: Opening sermon on Christ’s mediatorial glory
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Flavel's rich work displays Christ in all His saving offices — as prophet, priest, and king, and in every step of His humiliation and glory. It is a fitting companion to John's Gospel, which more than any other reveals who Jesus is: the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, the resurrection, the way, the truth, and the life. In warm and searching chapters Flavel opens the fountain from which all our life flows, moving the reader from understanding to adoration. Read it to know the Savior of John's Gospel more fully — and to love Him more.

10. Expositions of Holy Scripture: John 3 — Alexander Maclaren

Alexander Maclaren
Assigned portion: John 3 (Nicodemus and the New Birth)
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Maclaren, one of the greatest expository preachers of his age, opens the great scenes of John's Gospel with rare insight and beauty. Rather than treating every verse, he settles on the pivotal moments — the interview with Nicodemus, the woman at the well, the raising of Lazarus, the farewell in the upper room — and draws from each a rich and moving message. It is an ideal companion to the course for the reader who wants not only to understand these passages but to feel their power. Few writers can so open a familiar scene that it strikes the heart afresh; here John's Gospel is preached, and lives.

Romans

1. Commentary on Romans — Frédéric Godet

Frédéric Godet
Assigned portion: Introduction and Romans 1:16–17
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Godet's commentary on Romans is one of the great exegetical guides to the epistle — the work of a scholar of vast learning and warm evangelical faith. He follows Paul's argument step by step with rare care, weighing every word and tracing the logic of the whole. Fuller and more demanding than a devotional aid, it is the ideal companion for the student who wants to grasp exactly what Paul is saying, and why. For a thorough, believing, and even-handed exposition of Romans — fair to the difficulties and reverent toward the text — it remains, after more than a century, among the very best.

2. The Epistle to the Romans (Expositor's Bible) — Handley C. G. Moule

Handley C. G. Moule
Assigned portion: Introduction and Romans 1:1–17
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Moule was a scholar of the first rank and a man of deep devotion, and his exposition of Romans joins the two beautifully. Warm, clear, and spiritually rich, it opens the epistle for the ordinary reader without sacrificing depth, always drawing the mind toward worship and the heart toward Christ. It is an ideal companion to the course — a middle way between the technical commentaries and the purely devotional works. Read it to feel the warmth as well as the weight of Romans, guided by a teacher who loved the gospel he expounded, and longed for his readers to know its power in their own souls.

3. Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans (1835) — Charles Hodge · Reformed reading

Charles Hodge · 1835
Assigned portion: Introduction and Romans 1:16–17
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Hodge's commentary is the classic statement of the Reformed reading of Romans — thorough, closely reasoned, and doctrinally weighty. For generations it has been the standard guide for those who understand Paul's teaching on sin, grace, and election in the Calvinist tradition, and it argues that case with great force and clarity. It is included here as the fullest and finest presentation of that view, which the student should know well. Read it for a powerful and careful exposition of Romans from the Reformed side — and read it alongside the Wesleyan voices on this shelf, that you may weigh the great questions for yourself.

4. Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament: Romans — John Wesley · Wesleyan reading

John Wesley
Assigned portion: Romans 3:21–31 notes
Approximate length: 2,300 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Wesley's Explanatory Notes distill a lifetime of study into brief, pointed comments on the text, and his notes on Romans give the classic Wesleyan and Arminian reading of the epistle. Clear and warm, they hold firmly to the free grace of God offered to all, and to the reality of faith as the appointed means by which we are justified. It is included here as a fitting companion and counterpart to the Reformed commentaries. Read it for a concise, believing, and heart-warming guide to Romans from the tradition of the great evangelist whose own heart was ‘strangely warmed’ by this very book.

5. St. Paul's Epistle to the Romans: A Devotional Commentary — W. H. Griffith Thomas

W. H. Griffith Thomas
Assigned portion: Romans 5:1–11
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Griffith Thomas wrote for the working pastor and the earnest layman, and his devotional commentary on Romans is a model of clear, warm, orderly teaching. He lays out the structure of each section, explains its meaning simply, and applies it to the Christian life, so that the reader grasps both the argument and its bearing on the soul. It is an ideal companion to the course, especially for those who find the larger commentaries daunting. Read it for a trustworthy, balanced, and encouraging guide through Romans — one that never loses the great aim of the book: to display the righteousness of God, and to bring the sinner to trust in it.

6. Commentary on Romans — Daniel D. Whedon · Arminian reading

Daniel D. Whedon
Assigned portion: Romans 8:1–17
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Whedon was the ablest Methodist commentator of his age, and a vigorous defender of human freedom and responsibility under the grace of God. His commentary on Romans gives the Arminian reading with learning and precision, especially in the difficult chapters on election, where he argues carefully against a deterministic understanding of Paul. It is included here as a strong scholarly counterpart to the Reformed commentaries, so that both sides are fairly heard. Read it for a thoughtful and well-reasoned exposition of Romans from the Arminian side — and, with the other voices here, to think through the deep questions honestly and for yourself.

7. The Doctrine of Justification (1867) — James Buchanan

James Buchanan · 1867
Assigned portion: Opening chapter defining justification
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Buchanan's classic is the great work on the doctrine that stands at the heart of Romans — justification by faith. With clarity and care he sets forth what it means to be declared righteous before God, not by our works but by faith in Christ and His finished work, tracing the truth through Scripture and the history of the church. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on justification, the truth Luther called the article by which the church stands or falls. Read it to grasp firmly the central blessing of the gospel — and to rest, as Paul would have every believer rest, on a righteousness that is not our own but Christ's.

8. St. Paul's Epistle to the Romans: A Devotional Commentary — W. H. Griffith Thomas (Romans 12)

Octavius Winslow
Assigned portion: Romans 12:1–8 (Christian Living and Service)
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Winslow's beloved work is a devotional exposition of the eighth chapter of Romans — that mountain-peak of the epistle, which begins with ‘no condemnation’ and ends with nothing able to separate us from the love of God in Christ. With rare warmth and experiential depth he unfolds the believer's security, the Spirit's witness, and the certain hope of glory. It is the ideal companion to the course's unit on life in the Spirit, and a comfort in itself. Read it slowly, and let the great assurances of Romans 8 sink into your soul: that you are justified, kept, and loved with a love that will never let you go.

9. The Death of Christ — James Denney

James Denney
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on the doctrinal meaning of Christ’s death
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Denney's classic study goes to the throbbing heart of Romans — the death of Christ, by which God set forth His Son as the propitiation for sin, that He might be just and the justifier of him who believes. With power and clarity he shows that the cross is the center of the New Testament and of the gospel Paul preached, and that Christ died for all. It is a valuable companion to the course's units on the atonement and justification. Read it to understand why the cross stands at the center of Romans — and to marvel afresh that God commended His love toward us in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.

10. Preface to the Epistle to the Romans — Martin Luther

Martin Luther
Assigned portion: Complete preface
Approximate length: 2,500 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Luther's short preface to Romans is one of the most famous pieces of writing in the history of the church — the very words that, read aloud one evening, warmed the heart of John Wesley and set the evangelical revival ablaze. In a few pages Luther seizes the great themes of the book: sin, law, faith, grace, and the righteousness that is ours by trusting Christ. It is the perfect companion to the course's opening unit on the book that shook the world. Short enough to read in a sitting, it has changed more lives than many a longer book. Read it, and feel the fire that Romans has kindled in the church for five hundred years.

Galatians

1. Commentary on Galatians — Martin Luther (1535)

Martin Luther · 1535 Martín Lutero · 1535
Assigned portion: Galatians 2:15–21
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Luther's lectures on Galatians are the book that lit the Reformation's understanding of the gospel — that a sinner is justified before God by faith in Christ alone, apart from the works of the law. Fierce, joyful, and endlessly quotable, it is the great companion to our whole course; Luther called Galatians his own epistle, to which he said he was betrothed. Read it for its blazing clarity on grace, weighing its sharper edges as you would any single teacher.

2. The Epistle of St. Paul to the Galatians — J. B. Lightfoot (1865)

J. B. Lightfoot · 1865
Assigned portion: Introduction and Galatians 1:1–10
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Lightfoot gives Galatians the care of a first-rate scholar — the history behind the letter, the meaning of Paul's words, and the great questions of the early church — in a commentary still valued a century and a half later. Where Luther brings fire, Lightfoot brings light: read together, they let you feel the gospel's power and understand the text with precision, unit by unit through our course.

3. The Freedom of a Christian — Martin Luther (1520)

Martin Luther · 1520 Martín Lutero · 1520
Assigned portion: Opening half
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

In this short, radiant treatise Luther states the paradox at the heart of Galatians: the Christian is a perfectly free lord of all, subject to none, and yet a perfectly dutiful servant of all, subject to everyone — free from the law as a way of earning, freed for love as the fruit of faith. It is the finest brief statement of the liberty our sixth unit reaches, and among the most influential few pages ever written on the gospel.

4. Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners — John Bunyan (1666)

John Bunyan · 1666
Assigned portion: Sections 1–40
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Bunyan tells the story of his own soul — years bound under the terror of the law and his own sin, until grace broke in and set him free in Christ. It is Galatians lived out in one man's life: the passage from slavery to sonship our fifth unit describes, told with such honesty that generations have found their own struggle in it. No book shows more movingly what it is to stop striving to be accepted, and simply to be accepted in the Beloved.

5. The Reign of Grace — Abraham Booth (1768)

Abraham Booth · 1768
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on sovereign grace
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Booth traces the reign of God's grace from first to last — in the calling, the pardon, the justifying, and the final glory of undeserving sinners — showing that salvation is grace from beginning to end and never a wage for works. It gathers into one view the “whole of grace” our eighth unit sets out, written with warmth and order; read it for its exaltation of grace, letting Scripture, and not any single system, settle the finer points.

6. Christianity and Liberalism — J. Gresham Machen (1923)

J. Gresham Machen · 1923
Assigned portion: Chapter 2, Doctrine
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Machen argues, with unusual force and clarity, that the liberal Christianity of his day was not a variety of the faith at all but a different religion wearing Christian words — the very issue Galatians raises with “another gospel, which is not another.” It is the ideal companion to our closing units: a model of how to tell the true gospel from a counterfeit, and why the difference is not a quarrel over trifles but a matter of salvation itself. A reader armed with this book can recognize a false gospel in whatever new dress it appears.

7. Justification by Faith (Sermon) — John Wesley (1746)

John Wesley · 1746
Assigned portion: Complete sermon
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Wesley sets out plainly what it means to be justified — pardoned and accepted by God — and how it comes to us: by faith, received as a gift, not earned by anything we do. It is the same gospel Galatians defends, preached here in the clear Arminian voice our course leans toward, and a reminder that justification by faith is not the property of one party but the shared treasure of the whole believing church. Set it beside the Reformed voices on this shelf and see how they meet at the cross.

8. The Ministry of the Holy Spirit — A. J. Gordon (1894)

A. J. Gordon · 1894
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on the Spirit’s ministry
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Gordon unfolds the Spirit's work in the believer — regenerating, indwelling, sanctifying, and bearing fruit — the very life Galatians calls us to when it bids us walk in the Spirit and bear His fruit rather than the works of the flesh. Warm and practical, it matches our sixth unit's turn from bondage to the freedom of a Spirit-led life, and shows that Christian liberty is not licence but the Spirit's fruit growing freely.

9. Cur Deus Homo (Why God Became Man) — Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1098)

Anselm of Canterbury · c. 1098 Anselmo de Canterbury · c. 1098
Assigned portion: Book I, chapters 1–10
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Anselm asks why God became man, and answers that only one who was both God and man could pay the debt of human sin — the deep reason the cross stands at the center, in which Galatians will glory and in nothing else. It is the classic medieval statement of why the cross was necessary, written long before the Reformation and shared across the traditions; a fitting companion to our seventh unit, that we might glory in the cross with understanding.

10. The Marrow of Modern Divinity — Edward Fisher (1645)

Edward Fisher · 1645
Assigned portion: Opening dialogue on law and gospel
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Fisher sets law and gospel side by side in a lively dialogue, guarding the reader from two opposite errors — the legalism that turns the law into a way of salvation, and the licence that casts off the law altogether — the exact double danger Galatians confronts. Long loved in Scotland and beyond, it is a wise guide to the very distinction our course turns on: not law against grace, but each in its rightful place under Christ.

Revelation

1. The Apocalypse of St. John — Henry Barclay Swete (1906)

Henry Barclay Swete · 1906
Assigned portion: Introduction and Revelation 1:1–8
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Swete gives Revelation the patient care of a great scholar — the Greek text, the Old Testament echoes behind every vision, and the meaning of its symbols — in a commentary still consulted more than a century on. It is the reference work standing behind our whole course: sober and reverent, letting the book speak on its own terms rather than forcing it into a system.

2. Commentary on Revelation — Matthew Henry (1710s)

Matthew Henry · 1710s
Assigned portion: Revelation 1
Approximate length: 2,600 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Henry walks through Revelation with the warmth and devotional wisdom that have kept his commentary in print for three centuries, always pressing the text toward worship and holy living. Where Swete informs the mind, Henry warms the heart; read together they let you study the visions carefully and be moved by them, chapter by chapter through our course. (Henry reads the book along historicist lines, but his devotional riches serve every reader.)

3. The Letters to the Seven Churches of Asia — William Ramsay (1904)

William Ramsay · 1904
Assigned portion: Introduction and the letter to Ephesus
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Ramsay walked the ground of the seven cities and read their letters against the life of each place — its trade, its temples, its dangers — so that the words of the risen Christ to Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, and the rest come alive in their setting. It is the classic companion to our third and fourth units, showing how precisely each message met the church that received it.

4. The Martyrdom of Polycarp — early church (c. 155)

Early church · c. 155 Iglesia primitiva · c. 155
Assigned portion: Chapters 1–18
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

This early eyewitness account tells how Polycarp, the aged bishop of Smyrna, went faithfully to death by fire rather than deny his Lord — the very church to which the risen Christ said, “Be faithful unto death, and I will give you the crown of life.” Short and stirring, it puts flesh on Revelation's call to overcome, and shows what the book's promises meant to those who first read them under threat.

5. A History of the Work of Redemption — Jonathan Edwards (1774)

Jonathan Edwards · 1774
Assigned portion: Opening section on the plan of redemption
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Edwards traces the whole work of redemption as one grand story, from before creation to the last day, showing how every age moves toward the consummation Revelation unveils — the marriage of the Lamb, the new heavens and new earth, God all in all. It gives our course its widest frame: Revelation is not a puzzle set apart but the last chapter of the one story God has been telling from the beginning.

6. The Saints' Everlasting Rest — Richard Baxter (1650)

Richard Baxter · 1650
Assigned portion: Opening chapter on the saints’ rest
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Baxter dwells on the everlasting rest that awaits the people of God — the very hope Revelation crowns with its vision of the city where God wipes away every tear and death is no more. Written by a dying man for dying people, it turns the heart toward the new creation of our final unit, and teaches the believer to live now in the light of that coming rest.

7. The Parousia — J. Stuart Russell, 1878 (Preterist)

J. Stuart Russell · 1878
Assigned portion: Introduction and opening argument
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

The preterist school reads most of Revelation as fulfilled in the first century — the great tribulation and the fall of “Babylon” pointing above all to the judgment on Jerusalem in AD 70. Russell's book is its fullest classic statement. Most who hold this reading today are partial preterists, who see much fulfilled then yet still await Christ's bodily return, the resurrection, and the new creation — among them Kenneth Gentry and R. C. Sproul; a smaller full-preterist wing, which the seminary does not commend, denies a future return.

8. The Apocalypse: Lectures on the Book of Revelation — J. A. Seiss, 1865 (Futurist)

J. A. Seiss · 1865
Assigned portion: Lecture I
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

The futurist school reads Revelation from chapter 4 onward as mostly awaiting a still-future fulfillment — the tribulation, the beast, and the return of Christ yet to come. Seiss's warm lectures are a classic presentation, and this is the reading most familiar in the English-speaking world through the last century. Its best-known modern form is dispensational (John Walvoord, Charles Ryrie; the Left Behind series), though not all futurists are dispensational.

9. Notes on the Book of Revelation — Albert Barnes, 1851 (Historicist)

Albert Barnes · 1851
Assigned portion: Introduction and Revelation 1
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

The historicist school reads Revelation as a sweeping map of church history from John's day to the return of Christ, its seals, trumpets, and bowls marking real events across the centuries. Barnes's widely-used Notes give an accessible classic presentation. This was the dominant Protestant reading for centuries — held in various forms by many of the Reformers — though fewer defend its detailed identifications today.

10. The Book of Revelation — William Milligan, 1889 (Idealist)

William Milligan · 1889
Assigned portion: Introduction and Revelation 1
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

The idealist (or symbolic) school reads Revelation less as a timeline than as a portrayal of the great truths that hold in every age — the conflict of Christ and His church against evil, and the certainty of the Lamb's victory — rather than a forecast of particular events. Milligan is its classic voice. In modern times it is well represented by William Hendriksen, whose More Than Conquerors reads the book in overlapping cycles of this same conflict.

11. Against Heresies, Book V — Irenaeus (Historic Premillennial)

Irenaeus · 2nd century Ireneo · siglo II
Assigned portion: Chapters 30–36
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Historic premillennialism holds that Christ will return bodily before a literal thousand-year reign on earth, when the righteous are raised and creation itself is renewed under His rule. It is among the oldest positions in the church — Irenaeus, a hearer of Polycarp who had known the apostle John, sets it out here in the second century. In our own day it is defended by scholars such as George Eldon Ladd and John Piper.

12. Jesus Is Coming — W. E. Blackstone, 1908 (Dispensational Premillennial)

W. E. Blackstone · 1908
Assigned portion: Chapters 1–3
Approximate length: 2,700 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Dispensational premillennialism holds that Christ will come for His church before a coming tribulation and then return to reign for a literal thousand years, with a distinct future for national Israel and a sequence of prophetic events still ahead. It is the premillennial position that spread most widely through the English-speaking world over the last two centuries, and Blackstone's small book was one of its most influential presentations. In the twentieth century it was systematized by John Walvoord and Charles Ryrie and popularized by Hal Lindsey and the Left Behind series.

13. Christ's Second Coming — David Brown, 1846 (Postmillennial)

David Brown · 1846
Assigned portion: Opening chapter and postmillennial section
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Postmillennialism holds that the gospel will so succeed in the world that it ushers in a long age of righteousness and peace — the “millennium” — after which Christ returns at the end. Held by a number of the Puritans and widely in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, it receives a careful classic defense in Brown's book. Among its modern advocates are Loraine Boettner and Kenneth Gentry.

14. The City of God, Book XX — Augustine (Amillennial)

Augustine · 5th century Agustín · siglo V
Assigned portion: Chapters 6–9
Approximate length: 2,800 words. Read this portion only.
Why this reading matters

Amillennialism holds that the “thousand years” of Revelation is symbolic of the present age between Christ's two comings — during which He reigns through His church — rather than a future earthly reign. It became the majority view through much of church history, and Augustine's treatment in Book XX is its great fountainhead. In modern times it is well represented by Anthony Hoekema, Kim Riddlebarger, and Sam Storms.
